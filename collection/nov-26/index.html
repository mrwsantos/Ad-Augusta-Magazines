03Reflections by the Headmaster

“We live in a vastly different society from when Governor Grey founded the School in 1850, ‘for the welfare and educational advancement of persons of all classes and races who may inhabit this colony.’” Tim O’Connor, Headmaster

In 2026, Auckland Grammar School’s legacy is not a static monument to the past. The founding philosophy continues to guide and steer, but its legacy is a living one, through the words and actions our young men exhibit daily.

The legacy is lived through the choices they make — from wearing the uniform with pride to the respect they show each other and masters and their preparedness to contribute purposefully, whether it be in a classroom, on the sports field, in the performing arts or other extracurricular activities.

While we face considerable change in our sector, the principles of a liberal education on which Auckland Grammar School was founded remain integral to the School’s educational philosophy.

Inside our classrooms, consistency provides the essential foundation for academic progress, excellence and intellectual courage. Consistency of teaching standards provides the greatest opportunity for young men to achieve personal bests in their studies. Human behaviour thrives under stable, predictable conditions. When instructional routines are consistent across disciplines, cognitive friction is removed.

Consistent teaching ensures that expectations are transparent, eliminating the ambiguity and mixed signalling that elevate anxiety and undermine confidence. Confidence is one of the hallmarks of a Grammar man — that is, someone who works on expanding his belief in what he is capable of.

Like their peers, Grammar boys have some of the greatest freedoms outside our gates in generations and certainly in our School’s history. As a consequence, they also need the most guidance to decide what they will and will not accept personally. If they are to maximise the benefits available to them, we also need them to understand their personal responsibilities.

Through daily assembly messages, in our classrooms and on our sports fields, our young men can come to understand that they build personal credibility through acting with consistency — that is, people come to understand how they will respond across a range of circumstances.

When the majority of our young men understand these basic benefits of building personal credibility, we grow our culture while developing our high-purpose environment. Owen Eastwood would say, “In an inspiring group with strong standards and lived values, the line is high. Together, we take great pride in living above it. When someone falls below the line it is acknowledged and remedied.”

With these concepts in mind, the School may, to some, be seen as an educational outlier in our country. That perception is not a reason to change. Particularly, as we are prepared to back our young men to be representatives of the education we provide. If our emphasis remains on providing a rigorous education and our values are consistently lived rather than just cited, we establish high expectations as our norm in all contexts. Our legacy.

Recently, in assembly, I shared with the School Seth Godin’s sentiments that support our approach. He said, “What really matters is being better, and in order to be better, you need to be different. The thing about consistency is that it is different. The way to be better is to do the things everyone knows they should be doing but do them consistently. That is different. That is better.”

We take our responsibility to maintain a high-purpose environment seriously, as it helps shape how our young men respond to a variety of situations. This may include their approach to their studies, the way they compete in extracurricular activities, their ability to develop and maintain friendships, or their capacity to cope with pressure. If they are to be better, if they are to achieve personal bests, then our environment has to be intentionally primed every single day with energy, clarity, and trust. A high-purpose environment constantly connects daily tasks to a cause greater than the individual.

This is who we are. This is how we do things. This is what we expect of each other. Such an environment showcases young men who are embracing school life, endorsing our standards and showing that they are fit and ready for the task at hand.

It is a legacy worth living.

Per Angusta Ad Augusta.
Tim O’Connor — Headmaster

04From the Board

The School roll continues to grow throughout the year, and projections indicate sustained demand for places at Auckland Grammar School over the next few years. As a result, the School has not accepted students through the General Ballot for 2027.

The growth of the School roll remains a key strategic issue for the Board, which is considering ways to respond to increasing demand for a Grammar education without compromising the School’s character or the opportunities available to all students.

The expanding roll has emphasised the importance of our ability to collect the School donation. As the roll increases the School is increasingly reliant on payment of the donation. This enables the School to continue to provide appropriate facilities and resources for students and teachers. For this reason, I would encourage and exhort you to continue to pay the donation. To those that have paid, the Board thanks you for your contribution to the School.

Expanding and enhancing campus facilities remains an ongoing priority: both to accommodate roll growth and to provide high-quality learning environments for students. The Board continues to work with its advisers on the School’s Master Campus Plan, which sets out campus development over the next 25 years and beyond. This includes future planning for the Clive Road/Barnett Road Precinct, covering increased supply of teacher housing, an expanded Tibbs House facility and the expected purchase of 10 Clive Road (you can read more about the project on page 07 of this edition). This work is particularly timely given pressure on the Enrolment Zone and the need to attract teachers while ensuring the facilities at Tibbs House are maintained and enhanced to meet the needs of all boarders.

At this time of year, the Board recognises the outstanding contributions made across the Grammar community. Teaching and non-teaching staff contribute significantly to the School’s curricular and co-curricular life, ensuring students have access to a wide range of opportunities during their time at Grammar. Achievements in academics, sport, the arts, and cultural activities show that students across all year levels are making the most of the opportunities provided to them.

Now in his fourteenth year at Grammar, the Headmaster and leadership team continue to provide strong, forward-looking leadership. Tim’s ability to attract, retain and motivate high-quality specialist teachers, while ensuring the School is professionally administered, is central to maintaining our high academic, sporting and cultural standards. The Board seeks to recognise and encourage these efforts with the support of the Grammar community. Through your generous contributions during the year, the Board can support and acknowledge our students, teachers and support staff. We thank you for your support.

We are fortunate to enjoy enormous support from Old Boys around the world and the Old Boys in the United Kingdom and United States are particularly strong in their support of the School. As this edition goes to print, the Headmaster and the Director of Advancement Jeff Blackburn ‘67 have attended functions in London and New York where they will meet up with UK and U.S.-based Old Boys. These functions are important as they continue to strengthen the strong relationships between the School and the Old Boys living overseas.

I would also like to congratulate the editorial team — Amanda Booth and Jude Dobson — for their enormous contribution to this redesigned and enlarged edition of Ad Augusta. The magazine provides a superb overview of the many aspects that make up life at Grammar and the contribution of students, teachers, support staff and the wider Grammar community across a multitude of activities and disciplines from music, sport, cultural, academic life and the arts.

Per Angusta Ad Augusta.
Grant Trethewey ‘76 — Board Chair

05More Than Just a Game

Shaping character and values through sport.

Headmaster Tim O’Connor watching from the sidelines of the No. 1 field as the whistle blew on the 1A semi-final against St Kentigern College. Winning this game earned the 1st XV a position in the 1A final against Kelston Boys’ High School.

Beyond the scoreboard lies the true value of sport at Auckland Grammar School. Sport is a great teacher in life. Every match serves as a crucial classroom for building resilience, teamwork, and strength of character.

The Augusta Army on the sidelines.

On a bracing Saturday afternoon at Eden Park this winter, Head Prefect and Captain of the Auckland Grammar School 1st XV Nico Stanley led his team out to play in the Auckland 1A rugby final. The run out was a memorable moment. “It was a privilege to run out onto the hallowed turf with the team. We had fought hard to get there and knew the whole School community was behind us.” Deputy Headmaster Pastoral — Senior School and 1st XV coach Dave Askew has been through the sharp end of the season multiple times. “The last 1A championship was in 2014. Since then, the 1st XV has been in the 1A final twice, semi-finalists seven times, and in 2020, the 1st XV was leading the competition after five rounds, prior to the COVID-19 pandemic cancelling the remaining games.” Every season has a story, and the final pages of the 2026 one was about to be written.

The Eden Park occasion carried all that a premier school rugby final should: young men playing hard for themselves, their teammates, and their school. In the packed stands the blue and gold supporters included family, friends, students, staff, and Old Boys. The vocal Augusta Army were in full swing. It was an occasion that showed what sport can create: connection, identity, belonging and collective pride.

Unfortunately for Grammar, Kelston Boys’ High School prevailed 27–15. Yet, to reduce the day to that scoreline would be to miss its real significance.

The Lessons Beyond Results

Yes, winning matters. However Director of Sports Development Dave Mackay argues that the sporting programme at school has a deeper purpose. “The most enduring contribution is what it teaches young people about themselves and others when things become difficult.”

There is a lot to contend with in a game, well beyond the technicalities of the individual sport. On a field, court, track, waterway or golf course, students are asked to meet pressure in public. Beyond that, they must collaborate with teammates, respond to coaches, absorb setbacks, exercise self-control and continue when the result is uncertain.

While they learn the satisfaction of preparation paying off, there is also the more complicated reality that effort does not always deliver the wanted outcome. Dave tells students frankly that, “there’s going to be a winner and there’s going to be a loser. You need to understand that you could be going home with this emotion or that emotion. And it is how you deal with the emotion that matters.”

That applies not only to the final whistle, but also to the moments during a game when emotions rise. Dave asks players to distinguish between reacting and responding. “A reaction can be immediate, emotional and unconsidered. A response asks a young person to pause, think, and choose the conduct that reflects his values.”

It is a lesson that has relevance well beyond sport. Young men will encounter criticism, conflict, disappointment and unfairness throughout their lives: in families, friendships, workplaces and communities. Dave says sport gives them repeated opportunities to practise how they will meet those moments. “That means teaching young men to respond respectfully when challenged — whether by an opponent, a crowd, a difficult result or their own frustration. The aim is not to remove all pressure from their lives. It is to help them develop the character and judgement to meet pressure well.”

Sport, he says, “is a great teacher for the values that we’re trying to instil in our young men. It can be a concentrated version of life.” Students learn how to deal with pressure, disappointment, adversity, or feeling they have let a teammate down. These are not trivial experiences for a teenager. Handled well, they help develop resilience and perspective within a setting where coaches, teachers, teammates and families can help put challenges in perspective. “We teach virtues through behaviours and responses,” he says. “We expect our young men to behave a certain way and therefore respond a certain way to adversity and challenge.” The scoreboard, he says, only measures one part of a sporting day. “If we only focus on performance, we’re going to basically ultimately end up failing the kids. Sport is competitive at Grammar, and winning is a good result, but winning at all costs is not part of our ethos.”

Opportunity Matters

About 65% of Grammar — over 1,700 students — participate in school sport. The challenge is to create meaningful opportunity not just for the most accomplished athletes within that number, but also for those who are trying a sport for the first time, playing with friends or finding new ones, developing later than others, or simply seeking the enjoyment of being active and connected. That principle has shaped recent thinking around participation.

This year, approximately 130 Junior students trialled for Basketball. In previous years, a limited number of teams might have been selected, leaving many students without a place. Dave Mackay as Director of Sports Development and Ben Richardson — Head of Sport questioned whether that was the right outcome for students who had put themselves forward. Dave says, “they have gone out of their comfort zone to come to a trial. They are not at a practised level, but that’s not the point. The point is they want to do it.”

Working with neighbouring St Peter’s College, Grammar helped establish a Grammar–St Peter’s Basketball league. Students who were not selected for the top-graded teams were placed in teams with their friends, given uniforms and the chance to play in a structured competition for a trophy. Ben Richardson, the School’s Head of Sport, is pleased with the result and the new plans it has now prompted. “Twelve teams were involved, creating an opportunity for roughly 120 students who might otherwise have missed out. Student feedback from the initiative tells us that this is an approach worth developing, and plans are now afoot to offer this localised inter-school model in Volleyball, Tennis, Badminton, and Football over the next 12 months. To get started, we have been fortunate enough to receive funding from Sport Auckland through their Tū Manawa Fund to scale the Basketball league and introduce a Volleyball competition from Term 4 this year.”

It is a practical example of a larger belief: participation need not be reserved for the few. A student does not need to be a future professional player for sport to matter in his life. The benefits are physical — time away from screens, regular exercise, healthier routines. They are also social and emotional. A sense of routine, a structure that has some goals around it, an outlet to talk with friends after a difficult day, a group to belong to and a reason to keep turning up. It can give a student who might be struggling in the classroom another setting in which he can contribute, improve and be known.

Dave has seen that value firsthand, recalling students for whom sport has been an important reason to remain connected to school and to make positive choices. There is also the possibility to develop over time. “Talent is not always obvious early. A student placed in a lower-grade team at age 13 is not fixed there forever. He may grow physically, develop skills, discover commitment, become more confident, or simply mature later than his peers. There is an opportunity every year at Grammar if you work hard, and you are good enough, to climb up the grades.” The task is to preserve pathways and encourage effort, rather than prematurely defining who is — and who isn’t — “a sportsman”.

Sport takes students all over Auckland and all over the country. The entire Rowing Squad have a Summer Camp in January before the school year starts. The above image is taken on the mighty Waikato River near Whakamaru.

The Teacher as Coach

At Grammar, staff are expected to make a summer and winter contribution to extracurricular activities.

Dave says having a staff member coach students adds enormous value to the teacher-student relationship. “Those relationships matter because a teacher understands the wider context of a school day. A student who arrives at training tired, distracted, or frustrated may have had seven classes and competing deadlines, all before he puts on his training gear. A teacher as coach is well placed to see the person behind the player. That does not mean lowering expectations. It means understanding how to set them intelligently and humanely. On a day when students are exhausted, the best decision may not be to demand more intensity. It may be to change the session, get outside, walk and talk, or make space for the team to reset. Such choices reflect an educational rather than purely transactional model of sport.”

A Lasting Education

When the 2026 1st XV final did not end in victory for the team at Eden Park, their coach Dave Askew felt for them. “The players would have felt the loss sharply, particularly after an unbeaten season this year in both 1A and traditional games. Disappointment is an unavoidable feeling in competitive sport.” He reiterates that the game brings value nonetheless. “Going through what sports can throw at you is why it is more than just a game. Sport can be a great teacher. If you have the right people leading, it can foster the resilience needed to respond to variables outside your control and to undesirable outcomes that might hurt.”

The players earned the right to represent their school on a major stage. The Augusta Army demonstrated what school pride can look like when a community gathers behind its students. Staff, families and Old Boys helped create an occasion that will remain in the memory of those involved. And the students who played learned how to prepare, how to perform under pressure, how to carry themselves when the desired result did not arrive, and how to begin again.

1st XV Captain Nico Stanley reflects on not just the day at Eden Park, but the year. “While the result was not what we wanted obviously, the team has had a great season. We have learnt a lot — and not just about the game. We have grown together as a team, and many of us have made friends for life. Mr Askew helped us create a team culture we really enjoyed.”

Nico’s comments reflect Dave Mackay’s thoughts about the value of sport at Grammar. “It provides a place where young men learn to belong, to serve, to persevere and to become the people they hope to be.”

Headmaster Tim O’Connor values the researched opinion of Owen Eastwood, author of Belonging, whom he quotes from: “It is from the cues around us daily that we quickly establish what the true standards are within the group. It is not about posters on walls, and it’s not about words; it is about the way we see people behaving daily.”

Tim translates Eastwood’s statements to the Grammar sporting context: “It is how we speak to each other and how we treat each other when we think no one is watching us — at any time. It is about how we support our premier teams on sidelines, it is about whether we keep a blue head when representing the school, when the opposition has gone red head. It is whether we win with humility and lose with dignity.”

These are the characteristics that determine Grammar. Sport is an enabler to show who we are as individuals and as a school. Sport at Grammar — it provides so much more than just a game.

06Manners Maketh the Man

For the young men of Auckland Grammar School, good manners are not a relic of the past. They are essential to being a Grammar man — today and always. Cultivating manners and mutual respect are at the heart of building character.

Form 7 students and 1st XV teammates Mahonri Soifua (left) and Marlee Jacobs clean their boots after rugby practice.

Outside the changing rooms overlooking the No. 1 field, Form 7 students and 1st XV teammates Mahonri Soifua and Marlee Jacobs clean dirt from their rugby boots before going inside. It shows respect for those who maintain the space and those who use it next.

The Asian Tutoring Group meet every week where students support each other with school work. Deputy Head Prefect Matayas Thomson talks to former master Allan Faull at the Augusta Fellowship Luncheon — Matayas’ father Paul was in the 1st XV team that Allan used to manage in 1988.

At lunchtime tutoring, a student respectfully helps another make sense of their work. At an event, Prefects help senior Old Boys to tables and converse with guests. As the adage says, ‘manners maketh the man,’ and manners are the trademark of a Grammar man.

Headmaster Tim O’Connor says a school environment helps young men develop manners and he reminds students that it is not difficult to do so. “Manners are something everyone can acquire that require absolutely no talent. As a Grammar community they are something that every single person can achieve, and in doing so we build a respectful culture. Respect is one of our cornerstone values, but it needs to be lived, with consistency.”

Mahonri says Mr O’Connor’s daily assembly messages about standards are well understood by the student body. “He reminds us that knowing the right thing to do is not enough. You need to do it.”

People standing up for others who could do with a seat on public transport is a basic example of good manners, expected of all Auckland Grammar School students.

Mahonri, as a daily commuter on trains, says he has watched people stay seated when they really should have given up their seat. The behaviour sticks out as unacceptable to him and reiterates another of Tim’s messages: “Good manners should be common knowledge, but they are not common practice.”

Manners are part of the daily rhythm of school life — correct uniform, punctuality, listening, courtesy to staff and visitors, standing when an adult enters a classroom, holding a door open for someone. Such actions, Tim says, are not just old-fashioned formalities — they are demonstrations of character.

“Character rises out of the standards we set ourselves, and the courage to live them consistently. We know that accepting responsibility for your own words and actions builds independence and self-discipline. Others come to expect you to behave in a certain manner. That is, you build personal credibility.”

Marlee sees this in practice in his own life. “Manners go a long way at school, around the house, with jobs, and sport. On the Rugby field, that means acknowledging coaches and players, speaking respectfully to referees, and helping around the changing rooms. A ‘please and thank you’ is very much appreciated by coaches and players.” He also reflects what it is like to be on the receiving end of good manners. “It gives me the feeling that I have actually made an impact on someone.”

Tim says cultivating good manners is beneficial for a student’s personal growth. “When young men accept personal responsibility for their behaviour then every action becomes a vote for the person they want to become. They keep getting better.”

A well-mannered young man then goes on to influence the world around him. “They start making contributions that add to the quality of our society. We see news stories about people insisting on their personal freedoms, but seldom do we hear of people reminding themselves and others of their personal responsibilities. That’s a job we take seriously at Grammar and having good manners is an easy one to work on for them.”

Tim’s vision is to not only ingrain the trait as part of a school day, but to extend the use of good manners beyond the school environment. “When young men treat others with courtesy, consideration, and genuine care in all parts of their life, we are in a position to start a revolution outside our gates.”

07The Grammar Precinct

Purchasing 10 Clive Road is a cornerstone investment in the growing Grammar Precinct.

This vital acquisition secures the opportunity to be able to provide more staff housing, expand Tibbs House for boarders, and better cater for boarding masters with growing families.

Ryland Te Tamaki, Head Prefect of Tibbs House, plays pool with Freddy Cuming, son of Deputy Director of Boarding Matt Cuming. The staff that live in staff housing along Clive Road and Barnett Crescent value the community they have created together.

Almost two years ago, Auckland Grammar School decided to pursue the purchase of 10 Clive Road to become the cornerstone of a new Grammar Precinct, with the community at its heart. The purchase of this property will enable improvements to Tibbs House for the boarders and house masters who call it home. It also provides the possibility to expand staff housing within a much-enjoyed community surrounding the School.

In December 2024, knowing how valuable the 2,000m² property would be to Grammar, the owner offered it to the School first to see if there was interest in purchasing it, before taking it to the open market. The answer was a resounding ‘thank you for the opportunity, we would love to see if we can make this work.’

Neighbouring 8 Clive Road, at 1,000m² is already in Grammar’s possession. Beside No. 8, Tibbs House sits on a large site on the corner of Clive and Mountain Roads. Consequently, securing No. 10 creates a contiguous landholding, which Headmaster Tim O’Connor says is key. “10 Clive Road is a strategic purchase for the School. It is a multidimensional, legacy piece of land where location is everything. When the three properties are viewed collectively it unlocks all sorts of possibilities for us as a school community.”

The three sites offer considerable potential but realising that vision requires ownership of No. 10 by a trust entity at Auckland Grammar School by December 2026. And to own it, requires funds.

Funding the purchase

The agreed purchase price two years ago was under market value at just over $4.4 million. A generous Old Boy paid the $1.5 million deposit, which he has kindly since gifted to the School. Another Old Boy offered to pay the balance of the price to secure the property himself in the interim, so that the owner could move out and move on with her life. The generosity of these two men ensured the property did not go to the open market but would later be able to go into Grammar’s possession.

For the past two years, Auckland Grammar School has successfully fundraised towards the agreed milestones to pay back $2.9 million to the temporary owner of the house. If the entire amount can be paid back in full by the end of 2026, the property will be in the School’s possession.

The School’s Director of Advancement Jeff Blackburn ‘67 says the wider Grammar community has understood “that this is an opportunity that simply cannot be missed. As such, they collectively helped us pay back $1 million to the interim owner at the end of 2025.” The remaining $1.9 million needed to be raised by the end of 2026 has been going well, with $565,000 remaining to be found as at the end of September 2026. However, Jeff notes that the clock is ticking. “We now only have a couple of months to source that support, but we are hopeful our community will help make the purchase of 10 Clive Road a reality for Auckland Grammar School.”

The School community has always managed to fund vital things into reality throughout its history. From the World War I obelisk in 1922 through to the Te Ara library complex and the new pool in 2024. Tim says the hallmark of the Grammar community over time has been that “we take control of our own destiny through the active engagement of our community, feeding forward to provide us with the Auckland Grammar School we have today.”

Tim muses on a Greek proverb: a society grows great when old men plant trees in whose shade they shall never sit. “This proverb accurately reflects our community’s way of supporting a Grammar education through time. It is now our turn as we look ahead to provide future students, staff, headmasters and boards with the legacy purchase of 10 Clive Road, and the enormous amenity that will bring.”

School counsellor Justin Doolan and Academic Administration Manager Brenda Craw catch up with Director of Sports Development Dave Mackay. Rose Cuming, wife of Deputy Director of Boarding Matt Cuming, enjoys the community of friends that live around them, and the proximity to the School for Matt.

Tibbs House

The School’s boarding establishment at Tibbs House requires both upgrading of the existing facilities and the creation of new ones.

It would be good to give more space to boarders as they get older. While having four or five to a room might still be the norm for junior students, moving to single or twin accommodation for Form 5 and 6 students would be ideal.

Tim also has a vision for the most senior students. “I would like to see potentially five Form 7 students living in a townhouse-type environment with their own kitchen and laundry facilities. We could provide them with a meal kit once or twice a week so they could learn to cook their own meals. We always say we want to produce young men who can contribute to society, so we need to think about preparing them for that next step.”

The School also wants to upgrade the style of accommodation offered to housemasters, especially those who have young families. The current housing has a maximum of two bedrooms which Tim recognises needs to change as it precludes boarding masters with growing families. “We encourage having families live at Tibbs House because it provides a different feel to other boarding establishments.”

Headmaster Tim O’Connor outside 10 Clive Road. Part of Tim’s hope for the land use is to be able to provide purpose-built accommodation for Form 7 boarders at Tibbs House where they could be provided the opportunity to cook their own meals a couple of days a week.

A Community of Staff Housing

The Headmaster hopes the area could also provide more housing for teachers.

There is an ever-growing waiting list for the 17 existing rent-subsidised staff houses along the northern side of Clive Road and through Barnett Crescent.

Housing is an excellent way to attract and retain good teachers at Grammar given the cost of housing in central Auckland can be challenging. The proximity to campus also makes it easier to be involved in the day-to-day life of the School.

Biology teacher Matt Cuming and wife Rose have three young children and thoroughly enjoy living in the community that has arisen around staff housing. Rose says without grandparents in Auckland, “it can be quite hard, but everyone supports everyone here. All our kids hang out together and we get time with other adults. We don’t have to travel anywhere to organise a catch-up with mates which can be difficult sometimes with kids in Auckland. We just bomb around to each other’s houses unannounced. Our friends outside of Grammar are always so incredibly jealous of our set up. Our kids are genuinely never inside — they are always running around with other kids or out on the field. They also get to interact with the hostel boys, which they love.”

Senior Academic Dean Marie Gaspar is Matt and Rose’s neighbour and agrees that there is a lot to love about living close to the School in staff housing. “It is safe and there’s no stressing out about being able to afford rent. It feels like a real community and makes all of Grammar feel like home. Living here contributes to my overall happiness and is one of the key reasons that I don’t want to leave the School.” The proximity to her place of work is a real plus. “It makes life so much easier: no parking stress, no commuting. I have it all right at my doorstep.”

Rose says that being close by to work is also beneficial to both Matt and the family. “We love it that Matt doesn’t have to sit in traffic to and from work meaning maximum family time. That means he can really be a hands-on dad but a hard worker too. He gets the best of both worlds.”

Justin Doolan, a School counsellor, says it is a close and welcoming community where people genuinely get to know other staff and their families. “There is a strong sense of trust and care, knowing that we are here for one another and will always look out for each other. Whether it’s a barbecue, dinner together, helping a family settle in from overseas, or simply asking, “Have you got a spare egg or an onion?”, it’s the little things that create a real sense of belonging. We all have busy lives, but there is a genuine warmth, friendship and trust within our community that makes living here very special.”

Rose concurs with Justin’s summation and adds her thoughts as a mum to three young children. “I love having people around. Being a stay-at-home mum can sometimes be isolating, but I never feel alone because there’s always someone around. It’s a real village.”

10 Clive Road is a 2000m² site that runs between Clive Road and Castle Drive. It adjoins 8 Clive Road, which is already owned by the School. With No. 8 neighbouring Tibbs House (the School’s boarding hostel), the addition of No. 10 provides a substantial contiguous land holding.

Tim strongly feels that creating more staff housing will be a fundamental requirement for the School going forward. “If we are to retain the essence of Grammar, we need more passionate teachers living close by, so their job is achievable and rewarding.” With 2000m² available at 10 Clive Road, more staff housing becomes a possibility.

The Grammar Precinct

Tim describes ‘a Grammar Precinct’ as the most apt terminology for the amenity that 10 Clive Road would add.

He has mused on the possibilities this Grammar Precinct would offer. “What does a new precinct look like where teachers and their families are part of a wider boarding community? While Auckland Grammar School is a school with boarding, rather than a boarding school, could we find some middle ground? Why could we not lead the way? Why would we not create a Grammar precinct that attracts engaged and committed students and skilled, passionate practitioners (and their families) who are truly rewarded for the surplus value they provide to generations of Grammar students?”

Looking forward, a growing roll will equate to the need for more teachers, and more places at Tibbs House for out-of-zone students, which makes 10 Clive Road a sensible, and critical addition to the School’s property portfolio.

Getting into Grammar on the out-of-zone public ballot has not been a possibility in the past few years, and that seems unlikely to change with the current in-zone pressure, as Tim reiterates. “The roll size or demand for places at Grammar will not diminish. Our roll is now at its largest in our history, at almost 2,800. This year we have a cohort of 564 in Form 3, a Form 5 cohort of 608 students and a Form 7 with 490. We are in unprecedented times.”

Therefore, securing 10 Clive Road is timely. This key property will provide opportunities to anchor Tim’s vision for a growing Grammar precinct in the neighbourhood.

“Grammar has enjoyed a strong presence along Mountain Road since 1916, and that presence will not diminish. Instead, through the new Grammar Precinct, it will become stronger.

“No. 10 symbolises our aspirations for the future of Grammar. If we are to protect the essence of a Grammar education in 10, 20 and 50 years’ time, we need to begin accepting responsibility for this now. By purchasing this property, it will provide future boards and future headmasters with important choices around quality accommodation for our staff and evolving the boarding experience. 10 Clive Road is perfectly positioned to enable all of this.”

Just prior to the end of Term 2, Auckland Grammar School hosted its annual Matariki celebration. Led by senior Te Reo Māori students, the celebrations took place in Te Ara Mātauranga, with the playing of traditional Māori instruments and a special karakia delivered by Form 6 student Lincoln Evans. Whānau and friends who attended were able to develop and strengthen their understanding of this process.

Prefects Marlee Jacobs, Ryland Te Tamaki and Nīkau-Te Ha Thorpe and Form 7 student Victah-Levi Hopkinson delivered a presentation that identified the various sections involved in acknowledging Matariki. Information was also shared about how Matariki is celebrated in other islands such as Tonga and Sāmoa.

Guests reflected on the past and remembered friends and family who had passed on during the year. Whānau then experienced how the stars were utilised to make plans for the future, including Hiwa-i-te-rangi, the youngest of all the stars in the cluster, which is associated with dreams and aspirations. Following this, senior students invited everyone to the Heritage Room for refreshments.

14The Commerce Faculty

Understanding how business works.

Form 4 students learn about Consumer Law and Insurance from Business Studies master Doug Newton. Form 7 students Jonathan Hu (far left) and Aarush Kumar (second from right) both made the New Zealand Economics Olympiad team that went to Greece this year.

Head of Faculty — Commerce and Head of Economics John Wilson enjoys bringing academic learning into the real world for students. “I tell every student that they can’t avoid economics in life; it is everywhere.” He also reminds them that they make choices constantly about how they spend their money, their time, and what their priorities are, just as a business or government does.

The Commerce Faculty’s subjects — Business Studies, Economics and Accounting — give students the language and tools to understand those choices, whether they are considering the price of a purchase, the cost of borrowing, the forces affecting a market, or how decisions are made.

That relevance helps explain the Commerce Department’s enduring popularity at the School. While Business, Economics and Accounting offer pathways into the more obvious career fields associated with finance, entrepreneurship and business management, they also provide a valuable foundation for students whose futures may lie elsewhere.

“Many of our Commerce students will go on to complete Commerce degrees or conjoint them with another degree. However, I think exposure to some of the fundamental principles of Economics, Business and Accounting, even if only at Form 4 level is good for any student. The life skills that knowledge imparts are helpful. The government is aware of this too, with their wish for more financially literate students across the country.”

The journey begins for many students with Business Studies, Economics and Accounting in Form 4, one of the School’s most popular option choices. From Form 5 to Form 7, students can pursue each subject separately or combine two of the three. The faculty supports students working through NCEA and Cambridge pathways. Economics also attracts a notable number of Form 7 students who take it up as an additional subject alongside their established A-level programme.

Economics as a subject, John says, has broad appeal because it helps students connect classroom concepts to the world around them. “Students examine the behaviour of consumers and firms, the working of markets, macroeconomic questions and the role of government.” It is a subject that has a personal benefit too he adds. “We are all consumers. We all face opportunity costs and demands on our time, our income and our skills, and the subject gives students a structured way to think about those pressures.”

The subject of Business Studies brings a more direct focus on how organisations operate: planning, marketing, leadership, systems and decision-making. John has watched this subject grow hugely in popularity and importance at the School since its inception in the mid-1990s, both in NCEA and Cambridge.

NCEA Level 2 Business Studies hold an annual Market Day. The food-based businesses are very popular with the Grammar School customer base.

Accounting as a subject offers the more technical discipline of financial information and analysis.

The rapid rise of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and the place of it in education is a question every teacher must currently address. In the Commerce Faculty, John sees AI as a tool students will inevitably use, but he does not see it as a replacement for thinking. “You still have to understand the fundamentals,” he says. “There is no point having a calculator if you do not have a rough idea of what you expect in an answer.” Assessments have accordingly shifted toward supervised, in-class work, including handwritten responses and closely controlled digital tasks. The goal is not to reject technology, but to ensure it supports learning rather than substitutes for it.

The annual Market Day gives Year 12 Business students the chance to test their ideas in public. Students create a business plan, develop a product or service, establish systems, market their offering and then evaluate the outcome. Food and drinks are popular choices for student customers at lunchtime, but the most important learning lies in more than calculating profit. It is in the planning, execution and review of their product launch and sales. There may also be the ability to adapt when a real customer stands in front of them.

The Economics Department’s academic ambition was evident this year in international Economics competitions. Form 7 students Jonathan Hu and Aarush Kumar represented New Zealand at the International Final Round of the Economics Olympiad in Ancient Olympia, Greece in August. To get there they had to advance through national qualifying rounds that included multiple-choice testing, written essays and oral presentations. Competing across microeconomics, macroeconomics and finance, Jonathan placed 11th among 147 competitors, just one point short of a Gold medal. Aarush placed in the top 50 and earned Bronze. Their performances contributed to New Zealand finishing sixth overall, with the team collecting two Silver and two Bronze medals. Beyond the testing, the event offered students the chance to take part in an Economics Policy Challenge, a traditional Greek Glendi celebration and some sport with peers from around the world.

The School also had representation at the International Economics Olympiad in China, underlining the level at which Commerce students are engaging beyond the classroom. Selection for such teams is demanding as national representatives are among the strongest economics students in New Zealand. After a rigorous testing process, Form 7 student Toby King was selected as captain of the New Zealand team (you can read of Toby’s success on page 08).

Locally, the University of Auckland New Zealand Economics Competition has grown in size and reputation since its inception in 1997, becoming the largest and most prestigious competition of its kind for secondary students. It is a 50-minute examination paper containing 50 questions that test classroom theory and general knowledge of current economic issues.

In 2026, approximately 2,500 students from 69 schools throughout New Zealand entered the Competition. Auckland Grammar School entered 29 students across Division 1 (studying at Year 13) and Division 2 (studying at Year 12) and earned 11 High Distinction (top 5%), 14 Distinction (next 15%) and 4 Credit (next 20%) awards. Most notably, Form 7 student Jonathan Hu gained first in New Zealand in Division 1 and First in New Zealand overall with a perfect score of 50/50.

The Commerce Faculty connects students to the real world. They gain hands-on experience from basic accounting and running a hot dog stall to preparing for tertiary education and competing on the international stage. And as John reiterates, “studying Commerce gives students a more informed way to navigate the decisions that will shape their adult lives.”

Insurance for Good

Supporting the School through your business insurance.

What if there was a way to donate money to Auckland Grammar School but it did not cost you anything? In fact, it may even save you money. Such is the case with the association between the School and Raizor.

The initiative is called ‘Insurance for Good’ and the name says exactly what it means. For any new business insurance policy taken out with their partners Gallagher Insurance, 15% of the commission is donated to a nonprofit. For the Grammar Community, that nonprofit is the Foundation Trust at the School. The hope is that the wider Grammar community will embrace the concept and support the School through this ongoing, passive giving opportunity.

A handful of businesses have already come on board, donating $7,000 to date with nearly twice that already committed this year. Among them are past parent Richard Miles, founder of 3 Wise Men, who said the programme made supporting the School effortless. “Gallagher made the renewal streamlined and easy. The concept is a win-win for both parties and an excellent way to continue to support the School that had such a positive impact on our son.”

Carmen Platt has also signed on and says it delivers benefits for both the School and her business CFX Signage. “We’ve found Insurance for Good well worth doing. We get to check our insurance is competitively priced, with the bonus of a donation to the School, and it takes next to no time.”

Jared Thomas, co-founder of Raizor, is the connection point between the client and Gallagher. He said finding broker expertise and value for the client is not a difficult task. ”Whatever sector your business is in, chances are that Gallagher already has deep, specialist experience there. As one of New Zealand’s leading insurance brokers, they have spent decades building trusted, industry-specific expertise.”

Gallagher’s specialist areas include construction and development, architects and consultancies, transport and logistics, financial and professional services, manufacturing and commercial property to name a few.

So far, Raizor has donated $625,000 to non-profit organisations nationwide. If the School’s Foundation Trust became a regular entity for donations, Major Gifts and Bequests Manager Daniel Reddish says it would make a huge difference to the School.

“Having a steady stream of income coming in is obviously very helpful. If multiple businesses signed on, even if their annual insurance costs are minimal, the aggregated effect is good news for Grammar, and good news for the business too if they can better manage their risks and market-test their pricing. It’s an easy and passive way to give back and we hope our community supports it.”

25The School and ‘Captain Moonlite’

The Archives hold many unknown stories. This particular story began with an enquiry from Jennifer Forster, a great granddaughter of Charles Chamberlin Jnr, a founding student of 1869. Jennifer kindly shared with us a recently found intriguing note written by her grandmother, a daughter of Charles.

Charles, born in 1854, was the oldest of four sons of Charles Chamberlin Snr, who came from Norfolk in England with his older brother, Henry, and purchased land in and around Auckland. Included was the entire Ponui Island to the east of Waiheke Island, on which he and his family settled and farmed, and it became known as Chamberlin’s Island.

When it came to schooling, Charles Jnr, then about 13, was sent at the start of 1867 to the Collegiate School in Karangahape Road, near Upper Queen St, which provided boarding. This school had been established by the Rev Dr Robert Boyd Kidd, BA, LLD in July of the previous year. The name of the Rev Dr Kidd may well ring a bell since he was soon to become the first headmaster of Auckland Grammar School. He and his wife and two children had arrived from London in May 1864 on the 688 ton ship, Golconda.

Interestingly, he initially referred to himself as the Rev Kidd, LLB, which was all totally correct having been ordained in England and graduating from Trinity College, Dublin, with a BA in 1844 and an LLB in 1859. Once he became involved in Collegiate College he assumed the title of the Rev Dr Kidd although his LLD was not awarded until 1871.

Collegiate School was understandably small and initially Dr Kidd was the only master, but at the start of the 1867 year with a roll of 70, a second master, Joseph Flower, late of the Auckland Commercial School, was employed. A Dr Giles was also involved, delivering a weekly lecture on Natural Science, but of more significance to this story, a third master named Scott was also added to the staff. He was present and referred to twice by the Superintendent of the Auckland Province at the half year prizegiving in late June of 1867, at which Charles Chamberlin Jnr received first prize for the Classics in the Middle School.

Who was this Scott? There are many tales about him, but there are also some facts to work on. Andrew George Scott was baptised in July 1842 in Rathfriland, County Down in Ireland. He was a son of Thomas, a Justice of the Peace, and Bessie Scott, née Jeffares. As a young man, Andrew was described as “dark, handsome, active and full of high spirits”. However, he was also known for impulsive acts of mischievous violence. It is thought that he studied engineering in London and, having travelled to Italy for further study, wound up fighting with Garibaldi’s redshirts in Italy in 1860, but this may have been one of his many fabrications.

What we do know is in December 1861, Andrew arrived at Auckland with his parents and his brother, Thomas William, on board the 1,358 ton ship, Black Eagle.

It is understood that Thomas, the father, had come armed with a land order from the English Immigration Office, which made him eligible for a free grant of 40 acres within the Auckland province and while they waited for blocks to come available they resided in a cottage in Khyber Pass Road. Andrew is thought to have been taken on as a land surveying cadet by Major Heaphy, VC, the Auckland Surveyor General.

By 1863, Andrew and his father had taken up a block of land at Maungakaramea, near Mangapai south of Whangarei, building a homestead and breaking in land for farming. However, they eventually decided that the hard farming life was not for them and returned to Auckland, living in Newton Road. Soon afterwards, Andrew was employed by Dr Kidd as the third master at the Collegiate School.

In May 1867, Andrew and his parents were invited to a ball at Government House by the Governor, Sir George Grey, to celebrate the 30th anniversary of Her Majesty the Queen, so the family was held in some regard. However, at the December prizegiving in 1867, Dr Kidd referred to Mr Scott “having been disabled for some time.” We do not know what this refers to but it may have been a gentle way of smoothing over a potential scandal.

Returning to the “intriguing note” mentioned at the start, the daughter of Charles Chamberlin Jnr wrote about Andrew Scott, the third master: “One of the masters worked on the minds of some of the older boys and persuaded them to go with him to Australia. He was very keen for my father to go also, but he would not. Later that master became the famous bushranger Moonlight [spelt Moonlite by Scott] and his gang.” In another account, he was accused of enticing some of the older boys whose parents were wealthy to endorse bills, effectively loaning him money. When the bills came due, he was unable to pay them.

It is quite possible that both explanations were true, but a scandal appears to have been avoided by Andrew Scott running off to Australia and in July 1868 he was appointed a lay reader of the Church of the Holy Trinity in Bacchus Marsh, northwest of Melbourne. In March 1869, he was moved to Egerton, near Ballarat, but soon tired of clerical life, abandoning it in style by robbing a local bank of prospected gold and laying the blame on the manager and the local school teacher. In an affidavit in which “the robber” absolved the manager of any responsibility, he signed himself as the purposely misspelt “Captain Moonlite”. This caused suspicion to fall on the manager, who together with the teacher, was tried for the crime, but both were acquitted through lack of evidence.

Meanwhile Andrew went to Sydney where he lived off his proceeds until being arrested by Water Police in late 1869 for fraudulently buying a yacht called Why Not which, along with a young woman, he intended to sail to Fiji.

On 20 December 1869, he was sentenced to 12 months in Maitland gaol. He was released in April 1872, only to be charged with the Egerton gold robbery for which he received 10 years hard labour plus one year for briefly escaping while on remand.

He was released in March 1879, and began speaking at open-air meetings on prison reform and similar subjects, but with a small gang of accomplices he held up Wantabadgery sheep station near Wagga Wagga on 18 November for two days. He separated by force two children from their parents to use as hostages. Police finally attacked the homestead and two of the gang, one aged only 15, and one trooper were killed. Andrew and three others were found guilty of murder and he and one of his accomplices were hanged on 20 January 1880.

While awaiting execution in Darlinghurst gaol, Andrew wrote, among many letters, that he had travelled to the USA and fought in the Northern Army and then gone on to Australia, via a stay in San Francisco, but the Civil War had ended in May 1865, so this also appears to have been a fabrication.

On 17 May 1869, Auckland Grammar School opened in the old immigration barracks in Howe Street with Dr Kidd as the headmaster and Joseph Flower as a master. Initially there were 79 students, including those from the Collegiate School, which in October 1868 had moved from Karangahape Road into the building vacated by Wesley College located in Upper Queen Street. These students included, Charles Chamberlin Jnr and two of his younger brothers, George and Ernest Henry. Boarding facilities were provided in the now empty Wesley College building.

Andrew’s father, Thomas Scott, BA, was ordained at St Paul’s Church in Auckland on 21 September 1874 by the Right Rev Bishop Cowie. He became the Rector of Christ Church in Coromandel, where he and Bessie lived the rest of their lives. Andrew Scott was never directly involved in the Auckland Grammar School, but his association with Dr Kidd, Joseph Flower, Charles Chamberlin Jnr and other Collegiate students provides Captain Moonlite with an interesting link to the School.

The Girls of Grammar

From a student to a master at Auckland Grammar School.

A long-lost aspect of Auckland Grammar School’s history was the co-existence of girls and boys at the School in its early history, with girls attending as students from 1888 through to 1908. During this time many girls walked the halls of their separated side of the School in Symonds Street.

Even before 1888, the School had helped produce an illustrious female scholar, Kate Edger, who went on to become the first woman in New Zealand to receive a university degree and the first woman in the British Empire to receive a BA degree. There was no secondary schooling available for girls, so Kate was granted permission to attend the School in 1874. She graduated from the University of New Zealand in 1877 with a Bachelor of Arts, majoring in Latin and Mathematics and went on to become the foundation headmistress of Nelson College for Girls.

Dr Hilda Northcroft started at the School in 1894 and earned a Junior University Entrance Scholarship. She gained her medical degree from the University of Edinburgh and became a leading doctor in women’s medicine in the 1900s, and was the New Zealand representative to the International Council of Women for 10 years.

Women across the cohorts pursued a variety of different professions after their time at the School and at least three found their way back to Grammar as teachers, probably the only Old Girls to teach at the School in its 157-year history. They were Fanny Hutton (class of 1896), Edna Gwendoline Blosse Lynch (class of 1900) and Mabel Elizabeth Freeman (class of 1904). All three women started as teachers at Auckland Grammar School in 1918.

Edna Lynch, the youngest daughter of Thomas Lynch and Maud Margaret Houlahan, attended the School from 1900 to 1905 and was repeatedly placed top in her classes.

Edna won a Junior University Entrance Scholarship in 1905 and went on to complete a Master of Arts from Auckland University College in 1911, where she was awarded scholarships in every year of her study. Records suggest that she then moved to Northland, where she later joined Whangarei High School in 1915 as first lady assistant teacher before moving to Wellington Girls’ College from 1916 to 1917.

In 1918, for only the second time in the School’s history, female staff members were employed to fill the gaps left by men going to World War I. Auckland Grammar School had seven women join the teaching staff. Edna taught Latin across Forms 3 and 4, with the class of 4A in the 1918 Chronicle welcoming “Miss Lynch to further our knowledge of Latin”. She was also the Form Mistress for 3A and 4D. Whilst records are difficult to find, Edna eventually moved to Northland. She married James Frederick Street Briggs, a barrister and solicitor and their daughter, Frances, was born in 1923, but died aged just 11 years. Edna retired from teaching in the 1950s and died in September 1958.

Auckland Grammar School has a long, proud history of Old Boys and Girls coming back as teachers, helping forge the strong sense of school identity and culture that we know as “Grammar Pride”.

Five generations across almost 150 years. One family, one school. Since Charles MacCormick first walked through the gates of Auckland Grammar School in 1875 the MacCormick name has appeared on the School’s rolls in almost every decade that followed — through two World Wars, a court bench, university faculties, hospital wards, and rugby and cricket fields. The first generation included five MacCormick brothers: Charles Edward (1875), Arthur Deloitte (1875), William Alexander (1879), Donald (1880) and Evan (1896). Today that legacy continues in Professor Alastair MacCormick ‘57, Murray MacCormick ‘59 and in the many other MacCormick descendants. Theirs is a story of how a family and a school can grow together over time.

Alastair and Murray are in the third generation of the MacCormick family to attend Auckland Grammar School, with their grandfather, Charles Edward MacCormick, starting in 1875. Born in Sydney, Charles was the eldest of the eleven children of lawyer John Charles MacCormick and his wife Elizabeth (née Deloitte). John and Elizabeth migrated to Auckland in 1865, when Charles was just three years old.

After his time at the School, Charles went to Auckland University College (now the University of Auckland) to study law and was admitted to the Bar in 1887. He soon became a partner in the Auckland law firm now known as Cairns Slane and in August 1906 he was appointed as Judge of the Native Land Court.

Charles spent 34 years on the bench, before being appointed as Chief Judge in 1939 just shortly before he retired. In addition to his duties as Judge, in 1918 Charles became Chairman of the Native Military Service Board and in 1927 he was appointed to the Royal Commission on Samoa.

The obituary published in the New Zealand Herald in July 1945 states: “During his long term as a Judge, Charles displayed qualities of kindness, patience and understanding in his dealings with native people...Leading representatives of the Maori race paid glowing tributes to the esteem in which he was held at a special function held in his honour on the eve of his retirement from the Court in 1940.”

Outside of the courtroom, Charles was a keen sportsman. He was an Auckland cricket representative for 10 years. He was also the first treasurer of the Auckland Cricket Association, a former Administrative Officer of the Auckland Rugby Union, of which he was awarded life membership, and a President of the New Zealand Amateur Athletic Association. Charles’ brothers Arthur (1875), Hector and Evan (1896) were also keen cricketers, with Arthur playing for Otago and Evan an Auckland cricket representative. Another brother, Donald (1880), represented Auckland at golf.

Charles married Mabel Nichol in 1888, having four children — three daughters and son Kenneth, who started at Auckland Grammar School in 1905.

Kenneth was a distinguished New Zealand surgeon and military medical officer who played a central role in New Zealand’s medical services through both World Wars. He spent five years at Auckland Grammar School and graduated in 1909 with a University Entrance Scholarship, before completing medical school at the University of Otago in 1914.

Like many others in their final year at Otago, Kenneth volunteered for army service and enlisted as a Lieutenant in the New Zealand Medical Corps in 1914. He served in Egypt, at Gallipoli and on the Western Front in France, rising to the rank of Major. In 1917, Kenneth was awarded the Distinguished Service Order for outstanding courage while directing the evacuation of wounded troops under heavy artillery fire.

After his discharge from active duty, Kenneth stayed in London to complete his postgraduate training, becoming a Fellow of the Royal College of Surgeons (FRCS) in 1921. Returning to Auckland in 1922, he became a prominent general surgeon and maintained active involvement in the territorial army medical service throughout the 1920s and 1930s.

Kenneth would soon be called back to active service in World War II, but not before he gave back to his alma mater between 1935 and 1939 as Vice President and then President of the Auckland Grammar School Old Boys’ Association.

During the early stages of the war, Kenneth returned to the front line as Director of Medical Services for the 2nd New Zealand Expeditionary Force in the Middle East and was promoted to Brigadier. For his services to medicine and the armed forces, Kenneth was made a Commander of the Order of the British Empire (CBE) in 1941 and a Companion of the Order of the Bath (CB) in 1943.

Kenneth returned home to New Zealand and continued to serve his country post-war, becoming President of the New Zealand Medical Association in 1953-54 and President of the New Zealand Red Cross Society in 1961, before his death in 1964. Through Red Cross, Kenneth and his wife Joan started the Meals on Wheels service in Auckland. Two of Kenneth’s sons — Alastair ’57 and younger brother Murray ’59 — would follow in their father’s footsteps and become part of the fabric of the great School on Mountain Road.

Alastair remembers his first few days as a Form 3 student very well. “When you arrived at the School in the same uniform that the boys wear today, the tradition still stands! You are always amazed sitting in the Great Hall — I was just like any other Form 3 student; you sit there and gaze in awe.” Alastair and Murray both went through the School with strong academic records and were School Prefects. Both participated in the School Cadet Battalion, with Alastair being the Regimental Sergeant-Major in his final year.

After graduating, Alastair headed to the University of Auckland and earned a Bachelor of Science in Mathematics and Physics and a Master of Commerce with First Class Honours in Economics and was appointed a Lecturer in Economics in 1969. In 1970 the university gave him study leave to go the United States, where he completed his PhD in Management Science at Yale University.

After returning to the University of Auckland, Alastair took a prominent role in developing the new fields of business and management studies at the university. He was Dean of the Faculty of Business and Economics for over 14 years and then became Deputy Vice-Chancellor (Academic) from 1996-1999. Subsequently the government appointed Alastair to the Innovation Board 2010-11, the Callaghan Innovation Grants Committee 2011-21, the Tertiary Education Commission 2017-2025, and as Deputy Chair of the New Zealand Government’s University Advisory Group, which delivered its report The Future of New Zealand Universities in 2025.

Alastair has also had a long association with the School. He was Vice-President of the Old Boys’ Association for four years before serving as President for two years. Alastair has been a member of the Headmaster’s Council for over 45 years and, when the School gained its own Board in 1984, became a member, serving as Deputy Chair from 1990 to 1993 and then Chairman from 1993 to 1998.

Murray followed his father’s trajectory and headed to the University of Otago, where he also graduated with a Bachelor of Medicine and a Bachelor of Surgery. He held a lectureship in surgery at St. Bartholomew’s Hospital in London and is an Honorary Senior Lecturer in the Department of Surgery at the University of Auckland.

With a career that spans almost five decades across general, breast cancer and vascular surgery, Murray has pioneered several major procedures and medical programmes in New Zealand. He introduced advanced laparoscopic procedures, carotid artery surgery, and post-mastectomy breast reconstruction techniques into general use. He also founded Auckland’s first multidisciplinary breast cancer management service and led the rollout of breast screening programmes in Northland.

Both Murray and Alastair were recognised for their outstanding achievements in their professions with a Companion of the New Zealand Order of Merit for services to health (particularly surgery) and for services to tertiary education and the community in 2020 and 2022, respectively. The School also recognised their contributions to society, presenting them both with an Augusta Award (Alastair in 2019 and Murray last October).

Continuing with the long history and tradition, William Alexander (Bill) MacCormick started at the School in 1956. Named after his grandfather William Alexander MacCormick (class of 1879), Bill was at Grammar for only two years (Forms 4 and 5) before heading to the Elam School of Fine Arts at the University of Auckland, beginning in 1958. After early employment as an advertising artist, Bill started painting full time in the 1980s and is known for capturing the essence of rural New Zealand towns, historic architecture, and local landscapes. Bill’s early works included watercolours painted in the Tongariro National Park, and a special interest is the emotional power of buildings in the New Zealand landscape that tell of those who have gone before us.

Bill’s uncle, Ian Charles MacCormick (AGS 1929), was a prominent accountant and administrator in Auckland. He moved to Sydney prior to World War II, serving in the Australian Army. On his return to Auckland, Ian founded the Junior Chamber of Commerce. Later he became Secretary/Manager of the Chartered Institute of Secretaries, the Auckland Athletic Association and the Auckland Aero Club.

The connection with Auckland Grammar School extends beyond the MacCormick family name. Alastair’s wife Raewyn is the daughter of Philip Potter (class of 1918) and her brother Ross started in 1960. Philip played representative rugby for Auckland. He was the Managing Director of Auckland firm of Tanfield Potter, well-known as importers and retailers of fine china and cutlery. He served a term as Mayor of Mt Roskill. Ross was a Prefect and Senior Tennis Champion in Forms 6 and 7. He was a long-time Partner at Tauranga-based legal firm Cooney Lees Morgan until his death in 2022.

Both Alastair and Raewyn’s sons Andrew and John, as well as Murray’s two sons, James and Campbell, secured the fourth generation of MacCormick men, with all four Old Boys attending in the late 1980s and early 1990s.

Andrew and John followed in their father’s footsteps, entering in Form 3A and finishing in 7A. Both were in the 1988 Hockey XI that won the Rankin Cup. Andrew completed his medical degrees at the University of Otago and is now an Anaesthetist and Intensive Care Specialist at the Royal Melbourne Hospital.

John was first in class across his five years at the School, Captain of Hockey, first violinist in the School Orchestra and was named Dux in 1989. John was awarded a Girdlers Scholarship in 1990, allowing him to study at Corpus Christi College in Cambridge University, earning a degree in Mathematics. He was awarded University Blues for representing Cambridge, captaining the University Hockey Team in 1992 and was in the British Universities Hockey Team.

Returning to Auckland, John completed a Master of Science degree with First Class Honours in Mathematics at the University of Auckland, before taking up doctoral studies in computer science at the University of Oxford, where he also gained a University Blue for hockey. His DPhil thesis Stochastic Algorithms for Visual Tracking (2002) was selected by the British Computer Society for publication in the Springer Distinguished Dissertations Series.

After working as a research scientist for seven years at Hewlett Packard and Microsoft, John moved to Pennsylvania, where he is a Professor of Computer Science at Dickinson College. He has written four books on Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence. First published in 2012, Nine Algorithms That Changed the Future: The Ingenious Ideas That Drive Today’s Computers has been translated into eight foreign languages. While Andrew and John are now based offshore, they both keep in close touch with their family and their alma mater.

John’s son, also named Alastair, was able to enjoy a full Grammar experience in Forms 4 and 5, during the family’s 2023-24 sabbatical year in Auckland, when John was able to complete his most recent book on artificial intelligence. Alastair enjoyed his year of Grammar education, taking the opportunity to participate in many extracurricular activities, including Wind Band, Concert Band, Grammar Voices, Bridge, Fencing and Tennis.

Another fourth-generation member of the family is current Grammar Board member David Watt ’90, who is the great-grandson of Charles MacCormick’s younger brother Hector. David, with over 20 years of experience in the finance industry, is a Partner at Pencarrow Private Equity, New Zealand’s longest established private equity firm. Previously, David was a Managing Director in the Investment Banking team at Jarden. David has two sons at the School, Hugh and Max, and Max will become a member of the class of 2022 when he graduates at the end of this year.

Alastair and Murray’s daughters Judith and Catherine also continue the family legacy. Judith’s husband Warwick Stanes is a Grammar Old Boy from the class of 1988 and is the son of the School’s Archivist/Historian, Peter Stanes ’58, while Catherine’s son Max MacCormick is from the class of 2012, making him and his four cousins the fifth generation of MacCormicks to join the ranks.

Alastair is proud of his family history and their close association with Grammar over the generations. “Every generation has taken pride in being part of the Grammar community and maintaining the School’s core traditions. Our family’s Old Boys think it a privilege to have attended the School, to have had a Grammar education, and all remain deeply appreciative for the learning and values that stay with them life-long.”

Rt Hon Sir Kenneth James Keith, ONZ, KBE, PC, KC, ‘51

Sir Kenneth passed away on 13 May 2026, aged 88.

Regarded as New Zealand’s foremost international law expert and a highly respected and engaged member of the Grammar community, Sir Kenneth James Keith attended Auckland Grammar School from 1951 to 1955. He was Dean of Law at Victoria University of Wellington from 1977 to 1981 and later Professor Emeritus. He also worked in the United Nations Office of Legal Affairs from 1968 to 1970.

Sir Kenneth was part of New Zealand’s legal team in the Nuclear Test cases before the International Court of Justice and led the New Zealand delegation that prepared the additional Protocols to the Geneva Conventions in 1977. He helped shape the Official Information Act 1982, the Constitution Act 1986 and the New Zealand Bill of Rights Act 1990. As a member of the Royal Commission on the Electoral System, he also played a key role in New Zealand’s adoption of a mixed-member proportional (MMP) representation system of government.

Sir Kenneth was appointed to the Court of Appeal in 1996 and of the newly established Supreme Court of New Zealand in 2005. He was Judge of Appeal in Samoa, the Cook Islands, Niue and Fiji, and a member of the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council. In 2006 he became the first New Zealander appointed to the International Court of Justice, where he served a nine-year term. He was knighted in 1988 for services to law reform and legal education, and in 2007 received the Order of New Zealand, the country’s highest honour.

Sir Kenneth will be remembered as a distinguished Auckland Grammar School Old Boy whose lifelong commitment to justice and the rule of law has left an enduring legacy in New Zealand and around the world.

Stewart Alexander Matthews ‘48

Stewart passed away on 4 June 2026, aged 91.

Stewart believed attending Auckland Grammar School was a great privilege. He began his tenure in 1948 and distinguished himself both academically and on the sports field. He was a member of both the 1st Cricket XI and 1st XV Rugby teams for three years, captaining the 1st XV in 1952, and served as a School Prefect in 1951 and 1952.

After leaving School, Stewart studied at the University School of Architecture, qualifying as an associate member of the Quantity Surveyors Institute. He also served in the Territorial Army, gaining the rank of Captain and spending around ten years with the 3rd Battalion, Auckland Regiment.

Stewart’s business career began with Fisher’s Residential Aluminium Window Division before progressing to Alex Harvey Industries as Group Export Manager.

Known across Southern California as the ‘LA River Gate Maker,’ the work of Brett Goldstone ’72 has transformed the entrances of the city’s concrete water channels with his iconic ironwork.

Brett is pictured above with his first artistic gateway ‘the Great Heron Gates’ at Rattlesnake Park created and dedicated on Earth Day 1999. Commissioned by the Friends of the Los Angeles River, they were the very first artistic gates designed to welcome the public onto the revitalised Los Angeles River greenway.

Brett’s intricate metal sculptures depict native wildlife, river flora, the movement of water, and his ever-present signature addition of buildings. Combining artistic vision, industrial skill and environmental storytelling, his gates have become a key feature of the river-side community. The connection to the Great Heron gates from locals is so ingrained that the change from its original red to LA Dodgers blue in a recent refurbishment, saw locals paint a section of it back to red to note their disappointment in the change of colour.

Brett’s post-school career saw him try law, but the pull to travel and artistic endeavours soon saw him leave New Zealand. He was initially a cartoonist illustrating for publications like the L.A. Times before he learnt to weld and make industrial sculptures. He maintains studios in Los Angeles and Seattle, using both venues to mentor a new generation of industrial artists.

Despite decades building a life and career in California, Brett’s connection to Grammar has remained. When it came time to educate the next generation, he and his wife chose to send all three of their sons across the Pacific to his old school. Twins William ‘02 and Henry ‘02 became Tibbs House students, as did their younger brother James ‘12. All three now reside in the U.S.

Brett says the decision to educate his sons back in New Zealand was an easy one to make. “I wanted them to be exposed to the perennial values of a Grammar education - things like integrity, character, humility and good manners. These things go a long way in life and transcend generations and geography.”

Professor John Maccormick ‘85

Professor John MacCormick ‘85 has combined a lifelong passion for computer science with a commitment to education in his new book, Thinking AI: How Artificial Intelligence Emulates Human Understanding. Written for a general audience, the book explains how modern artificial intelligence works while exploring the enduring philosophical question of whether computers can truly think like humans.

John describes the book as having two aims: to make the complex ideas behind AI, including deep neural networks and reinforcement learning, accessible to everyone, and to examine the nature of human and machine intelligence. The project took six years to complete and evolved significantly following the release ofChatGPT in 2022, requiring substantial revisions to reflect therapid advances in AI technology.

Now Professor of Computer Science at Dickinson College in Pennsylvania, John sees education as his life’s mission. His academic journey began at Auckland Grammar School, where he graduated as Dux in 1989. He credits Mathematics teachers Ramesh Patel and Rory Barrett with inspiring his path into mathematics and, ultimately, artificial intelligence. After winning the Girdlers’ Scholarship to Corpus Christi College, Cambridge,John completed degrees at Cambridge, the University of Aucklandand the University of Oxford.

John completed the manuscript while on research sabbatical in Auckland as a Visiting Academic at the University of Auckland during 2024 and 2025. His return also marked a significant family milestone. During this time, John’s son, Alastair MacCormick Mitchell, attended Auckland Grammar School for Forms 4 and 5, continuing a remarkable family tradition. In doing so, Alastair became the fifth consecutive generation of the MacCormick family to attend the School, following a direct lineage stretching back to the 19th century.

Through Thinking AI, John hopes to help readers better understand one of the defining technologies of our age while encouraging thoughtful discussion about its impact on society and the future.

Five Old Boys represented New Zealand at the World Junior Ultimate Championships in Spain in July, helping the Under 20 team achieve an historic fourth-place finish. The result is the highest ever by a New Zealand Under 20 team, and the best performance at any Disc Ultimate World Championships in any division or age group since 2003.

Finlay Armstrong, Troy Craddock, Luke Dillon-Price, Cooper McCone and River Read-Smith (all from the class of 2021) played in the team’s first two games against Canada (5-13)

and the Czech Republic (10-12). The losses left them with little room for error, but Team Katipō responded in style, defeating Sweden, the Netherlands, and Singapore to reach the quarter-finals.

In the knockout rounds, New Zealand displayed remarkable composure under pressure. They edged Japan 13-12 in a dramatic universe point quarter-final before narrowly losing to the United States. With a place in the semi-finals at stake, Katipō again prevailed on a universe point, defeating Italy 13-12 to become the first New Zealand Under 20 team to reach the final four of a World Championship.

After an 8-13 loss to the United States, the team faced Japan in the playoff for third and fourth place, where River had some inspirational words. “To compete at such a high level with the pressure from these high level teams is a privilege. For us to go where no New Zealand team has gone before means we must do what no New Zealand team has done before.” The Katipō team’s fourth place finish meant they had already secured its place in New Zealand Disc Ultimate history.

Luke was proud of his fellow Old Boys who put in the mahi. “To finish so highly is a sign of the hard work every person put into this team - I couldn’t be more proud of the people I could share this achievement with.” For these five Old Boys, having discovered Disc Ultimate while still in Form 4 then to enjoy both national and international success together is a testament to the teamwork, discipline and competitive mindset that have underpinned their achievements.

Cyclist Aaron Gate ‘04 realised a lifelong ambition this year when he made his debut in the 113th edition of the Tour de France, riding for the XDS Astana Team, in late July.

Aaron rode primarily in the role of domestique, sacrificing his own chances of individual success to support his teammates through protecting team leaders from the wind, helping to control the peloton,and positioning riders before crucial climbs and sprint finishes.

Although the Tour de France is often remembered for its individual champions, success depends on the dedication of riders like Aaron, whose selfless work is essential to a team’s performance. Hefinished in 121st place overall, with a total time deficit of 5 hoursand 28 minutes. Despite suffering an early crash in the opening week, Aaron proudly completed all 21 stages, securing a notable 10th place finish on Stage 17.

Competing against the world’s best cyclists over thousands of kilometres and a wide variety of terrains, Aaron demonstrated the resilience, professionalism and determination that have defined his career. Having already achieved success on the track and the road, his Tour de France debut represents another outstanding milestone for New Zealand cycling and provides an inspiring example of perseverance, teamwork and excellence on the sport’s biggest stage.

With a mixture of nationalities in the XDS Astana team, including a German, Italians and a rider from Kazakhstan, Aaron was proud to be representing New Zealand in the iconic race, even if it meant missing out on defending his title at the Commonwealth Games in Glasgow. “It wasn’t easy. But in this sport, you have to try and grab opportunities with both hands. And a shot at doing a Tour de France was something that all cyclists dream of - I certainly have since I was just getting started.

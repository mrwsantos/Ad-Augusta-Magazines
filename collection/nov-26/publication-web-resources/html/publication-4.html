More Than Just a Game

Shaping character and values through sport.

Beyond the scoreboard lies the true value of sport at Auckland Grammar School. Sport is a great teacher in life. Every match serves as a crucial classroom for building resilience, teamwork, and strength of character.

On a bracing Saturday afternoon at Eden Park this winter, Head Prefect and Captain of the Auckland Grammar School 1st XV Nico Stanley led his team out to play in the Auckland 1A rugby final. The run out was a memorable moment. “It was a privilege to run out onto the hallowed turf with the team. We had fought hard to get there and knew the whole School community was behind us.” Deputy Headmaster Pastoral - Senior School and 1st XV coach Dave Askew has been through the sharp end of the season multiple times. “The last 1A championship was in 2014. Since then, the 1st XV has been in the 1A final twice, semi-finalists seven times, and in 2020, the 1st XV was leading the competition after five rounds, prior to the COVID-19 pandemic cancelling the remaining games.” Every season has a story, and the final pages of the 2026 one was about to be written.

The Eden Park occasion carried all that a premier school rugby final should: young men playing hard for themselves, their teammates, and their school. In the packed stands the blue and gold supporters included family, friends, students, staff, and Old Boys. The vocal Augusta Army were in full swing. It was an occasion that showed what sport can create: connection, identity, belonging and collective pride.

Unfortunately for Grammar, Kelston Boys’ High School prevailed 27-15. Yet, to reduce the day to that scoreline would be to miss its real significance.

The Lessons Beyond Results

Yes, winning matters. However Director of Sports Development Dave Mackay argues that the sporting programme at school has a deeper purpose. “The most enduring contribution is what it teaches young people about themselves and others when things become difficult.”

There is a lot to contend with in a game, well beyond the technicalities of the individual sport. On a field, court, track, waterway or golf course, students are asked to meet pressure in public. Beyond that, they must collaborate with teammates, respond to coaches, absorb setbacks, exercise self-control and continue when the result is uncertain.

While they learn the satisfaction of preparation paying off, there is also the more complicated reality that effort does not always deliver the wanted outcome. Dave tells students frankly that, “there’s going to be a winner and there’s going to be a loser. You need to understand that you could be going home with this emotion or that emotion. And it is how you deal with the emotion that matters.”

That applies not only to the final whistle, but also to the moments during a game when emotions rise. Dave asks players to distinguish between reacting and responding. “A reaction can be immediate, emotional and unconsidered. A response asks a young person to pause, think, and choose the conduct that reflects his values.”

It is a lesson that has relevance well beyond sport. Young men will encounter criticism, conflict, disappointment and unfairness throughout their lives: in families, friendships, workplaces and communities. Dave says sport gives them repeated opportunities to practise how they will meet those moments. “That means teaching young men to respond respectfully when challenged - whether by an opponent, a crowd, a difficult result or their own frustration. The aim is not to remove all pressure from their lives. It is to help them develop the character and judgement to meet pressure well.”

Sport, he says, “is a great teacher for the values that we’re trying to instil in our young men. It can be a concentrated version of life.” Students learn how to deal with pressure, disappointment, adversity, or feeling they have let a teammate down.

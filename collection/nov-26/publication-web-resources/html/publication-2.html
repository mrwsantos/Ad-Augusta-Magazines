NOVEMBER 2026

Reflections bythe Headmaster

We live in a vastly different society from when Governor Grey founded the School in 1850, “for the welfare and educational advancement of persons of all classes and races who may inhabit this colony.”

In 2026, Auckland Grammar School’s legacy is not a static monument to the past. The founding philosophy continues to guide and steer, but its legacy is a living one, through the words and actions our young men exhibit daily.

The legacy is lived through the choices they make - from wearing the uniform with pride to the respect they show each other and masters and their preparedness to contribute purposefully, whether it be in a classroom, on the sports field, in the performing arts or other extracurricular activities.

While we face considerable change in our sector, the principles of a liberal education on which Auckland Grammar School was founded remain integral to the School’s educational philosophy.

Inside our classrooms, consistency provides the essential foundation for academic progress, excellence and intellectual courage. Consistency of teaching standards provides the greatest opportunity for young men to achieve personal bests in their studies. Human behaviour thrives under stable, predictable conditions. When instructional routines are consistent across disciplines, cognitive friction is removed.

Consistent teaching ensures that expectations are transparent, eliminating the ambiguity and mixed signalling that elevate anxiety and undermine confidence. Confidence is one of the hallmarks of a Grammar man - that is, someone who works on expanding his belief in what he is capable of.

Like their peers, Grammar boys have some of the greatest freedoms outside our gates in generations and certainly in our School’s history. As a consequence, they also need the most guidance to decide what they will and will not accept personally. If they are to maximise the benefits available to them, we also need them to understand their personal responsibilities.

Through daily assembly messages, in our classrooms and on our sports fields, our young men can come to understand that they build personal credibility through acting with consistency - that is, people come to understand how they will respond across a range of circumstances.

When the majority of our young men understand these basic benefits of building personal credibility, we grow our culture while developing our high-purpose environment. Owen Eastwood would say, “In an inspiring group with strong standards and lived values, the line is high. Together, we take great pride in living above it. When someone falls below the line it is acknowledged and remedied.”

With these concepts in mind, the School may, to some, be seen as an educational outlier in our country. That perception is not a reason to change. Particularly, as we are prepared to back our young men to be representatives of the education we provide. If our emphasis remains on providing a rigorous education and our values are consistently lived rather than just cited, we establish high expectations as our norm in all contexts. Our legacy.

Recently, in assembly, I shared with the School Seth Godin’s sentiments that support our approach. He

said, “What really matters is being better, and in order to be better, you need to be different. The thing about consistency is that it is different. The way to be better is to do the things everyone knows they should be doing but do them consistently. That is different. That is better.”

We take our responsibility to maintain a high-purpose environment seriously, as it helps shape how our young men respond to a variety of situations. This may include their approach to their studies, the way they compete in extracurricular activities, their ability to develop and maintain friendships, or their capacity to cope with pressure. If they are to be better, if they are to achieve personal bests, then our environment has to be intentionally primed every single day with energy, clarity, and trust. A high-purpose environment constantly connects daily tasks to a cause greater than the individual.

This is who we are. This is how we do things. This is what we expect of each other. Such an environment showcases young men who are embracing school life, endorsing our standards and showing that they are fit and ready for the task at hand.

It is a legacy worth living.

Per Angusta Ad Augusta.

Tim O’ConnorHeadmaster

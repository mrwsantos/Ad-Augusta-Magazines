In Jackspeak - Navy Slang - it is “Bravo Zulu / BZ” or “well done” to Quinlan Te Tamaki ‘19. Quinlan graduated from Junior Officer Common Training (JOCT) 25/2 in the New Zealand Defence Force (NZDF) in December last year. Since then he has served on HMNZS Canterbury before embarking on specialist training. For Quinlan, that is becoming a Warfare Officer in the Royal New Zealand Navy (RNZN).

Joining an organisation that promotes esprit de corps, much like Grammar does, sits well with Midshipman Te Tamaki. “One of the things I’ve most enjoyed about the Navy is the strong sense of whanaungatanga and shared purpose. I’ve always appreciated having structure and clear expectations, and the Navy provides that while also challenging you to improve every day. It’s also a values-based organisation, where integrity, commitment, and looking out for your shipmates really matter. That reminds me of my time at Auckland Grammar School, being part of a community built around shared standards, pride, and supporting one another.”

Quinlan was Head Prefect in 2023, sharpening his leadership skills. “At Grammar I learnt the value of hard work, commitment and collaborating with others which has translated well to the NZDF.”

Encouraging leadership is a key focus for the NZDF. As such, there is a Leadership Award for every JOCT course. The criteria is for someone who has shown through their behaviour, performance and results to be highly effective at leading and influencing others. They have assumed responsibility for their own development as a leader and displayed the potential to achieve peak performance as a future leader in the RNZN. Quinlan was the 2026 recipient of this award for his course.

For any current Grammar student looking to join the Navy, the School has an award that supports that desire. The award comes with a one-off payment of $2,000. The H. J. Johnny Bull Award is granted to a Senior student who intends to enter the RNZN. To be eligible they must be a Form 6 or Form 7 student who has already commenced the application process for

a career in the Navy (preferably having completed the aptitude test). The award is administered by Sam van Deventer, the Student Services Office Manager at the School. Sam says they consider applications each October and welcome any enquiries at any time.

The award honours the commitment to service that many Old Boys gave to the Navy during wartime, in particular Lieutenant H. J. Johnny Bull ‘28. Johnny (and fellow Old Boy Tim Hill ‘34 whose story is on the opposite page) escaped the fall of Singapore in 1942. Johnny’s story is told in the book Close to the Wind by David Hill, a copy of which is available in the School library.

Unfortunately for Quinlan, his timing was such that he applied to the RNZN after he left school, so he was not eligible for the award. He encourages senior students at Grammar to look at the Navy as a career path and take advantage of the Bull Award. “If you’re looking for challenge, responsibility and the chance to be part of something bigger than yourself, this is it.”

Serving in the 1940s

The remarkable survival of Naval veteran Tim Hill ‘34.

David Hill reflects on the tenacious character of his Godfather, Trevor Neville ‘Tim’ Hill ‘34 (above, no relation). It was a trait that helped him survive WW II.

Tim Hill ‘34 attended Auckland Grammar School, as did his brothers Graham ‘33, Ross ‘36 and Bruce ‘46

He held a distinguished sporting record at Grammar. Tim won the School steeplechase, the half mile, the Donald Lane Cup for the one mile, and three weights in the school boxing championships, gaining the Watson Cup for the most scientific boxer in two successive years. He won the Auckland Secondary Schools’ half mile and mile championships and was second in the New Zealand junior mile. He played Rugby at every year level, reaching the 1st XV in 1938, and was a keen yachtsman, crewing on the C class yacht ‘Laloma.’

Athleticism was prized in the family, with his father George Hill ‘03 competing in Athletics at the Stockholm Olympics in 1912.

With the outbreak of war, Tim enlisted in the Royal Navy Volunteer Reserve (A1887) in March 1940. After his initial training at the New Zealand naval base at Devonport, Tim was sent to the UK for training, then to the ‘Alynbank’ - a merchant ship converted into a floating anti-aircraft defence vessel. After eight months he was sent to the Coastal Defence at Fort William in Scotland for training in small boat handling and gunnery, then was drafted to join a Flotilla being assembled in defence of Singapore. Tim’s convoy left Britain on 12 November 1941, and by the time it reached Singapore on 14 January 1942, the Malayan Campaign was virtually lost and the enemy was within artillery range of the city

In the month that followed, Tim served on ML311, a Fairmile patrol boat delivering commandos and rescuing retreating soldiers from behind the enemy lines. When the moment came to evacuate the beleaguered city, ML311 and another Fairmile ML310 were tasked with evacuating eminent members of the General Staff. ML310 was under the command of another Old Boy, Sub-Lt Johnny Bull ‘28, renowned as the perpetrator of the Great Escape. None of the 60 or more vessels that attempted to escape Singapore on Friday 13 February 1942 survived. ML311 was intercepted by a larger enemy force and blown out of the water. Of the over 50 men on board, barely 14 escaped with their lives. Tim Hill was among them.

After managing to get ashore, he spent five days hiding in mangroves. Supported by villagers, a group attempted to escape in a lifeboat that had been washed ashore, making their way cautiously along the Sumatran coast until they were again attacked by the enemy. The survivors of that skirmish, including Tim, rowed on, evading the enemy. They crossed the Sunda Strait to Java where, near Labuan, they were finally captured and became prisoners of war. This story is told by one of the men involved, Australian Rohan Rivett, in his book ‘Behind Bamboo.’

As a prisoner of war (POW) Tim survived the notorious Changi Prison camp in Singapore and later the Burma-Thailand railway (often called the Death Railway). From October 1942 to October 1943 the Japanese army forced about 60,000 Allied POWs and roughly 200,000 civilians (mostly Burmese and Malayans) to build a railway linking Thailand and Burma. Tim was in Camp 5 in Moulmein, the region that had the highest death rates due to disease, malnutrition, and brutal treatment. His survival was likely due to his competitive nature and physical resilience.

When Tim was repatriated in 1946, his sweetheart was standing on the Wellington wharf with a wedding dress over her arm. They were married forthwith. Tim and his brother Graham became successful seed and grain merchants in Te Aroha, before Tim’s early death in the late 1980’s.

Tim’s wartime survival remains inspirational. Peter Stanes ‘58, the School’s Archivist / Historian, has compiled a list of the Old Boys who served with the Navy and Merchant Navy in WW II who were not so fortunate. 42 Old Boys are sadly on that list. Lest we forget.

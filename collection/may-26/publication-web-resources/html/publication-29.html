For a young skier growing up in New Zealand, there are not always many chances to compete for one’s school. So, when Ben Barclay ‘15 had the opportunity to represent Auckland Grammar School at the 2018 North Island Secondary Schools’ Skiing Championships at Turoa, it was a memorable occasion. “It was an amazing experience to compete on my home mountain, alongside the rest of the School’s Ski team.”

Now based in Wānaka, Ben lives and trains in what has become one of the spiritual homes of New Zealand snow sports. Training is not always dictated by a neat timetable. Rather, it is shaped by the mountain itself. Days are planned around weather windows, snow conditions, and storm cycles, with sessions spread between Cardrona Alpine Resort and Treble Cone, skiing alongside many of his teammates. “It is an amazing, close-knit community of like-minded people.”

Ben wore the fern for the first time in his Olympic Games debut in Beijing in 2022. Having qualified just two weeks prior, Ben finished 10th in the Slopestyle event. His second Olympic Games earlier this year in Milano Cortina was even more memorable, when Ben was named Pou Hāpai Tāne (the male flag bearer) for New Zealand at the opening ceremony alongside Zoi Sadowski-Synnott. It was an honour he describes with genuine humility.

“I was tremendously honoured. Naturally - heading into an Olympic Games - you have goals and achievements that you set for yourself and want to achieve. However, being a flag bearer isn’t really one of those, as it is something not related to the sporting events themselves and is out of your control.”

Looking back now, Ben sees it as one of those rare moments in life that will remain vivid forever. “Now that the Games are finished, I look back on the experience of wearing the kākahu into the opening ceremony with the whole team with incredible fondness. That is a moment that will stick with me forever.”

Hamish Kerr ‘10

Hamish Kerr ‘10 with the Supreme Award trophy from the 63rd ISPS HANDA Halberg Awards held in February.

The annual Halberg Awards is New Zealand’s premiere event recognising and honouring outstanding achievements in New Zealand sports. At the ceremony held in February, it was Old Boy Hamish Kerr ‘10 who was named as Sportsman of the Year for a second consecutive year - and was then presented with New Zealand’s highest sporting honour, the Supreme Award. The accolade recognised not only his dominance on the global stage, but also his growing influence within New Zealand sport.

Hamish had a stellar 2025 season. He won the Diamond League Final in Zurich with a second-best jump of 2.32m, before winning Gold at the World Athletics Championships in Tokyo. He not only equalled his own New Zealand and Oceania record of 2.36m, but he was also the first New Zealander to win a World Championship medal in High Jump.

According to a Stuff article from September 2025, Hamish completed the ‘unofficial high jump career grand slam’, winning the Olympic Games, Commonwealth Games, the World Indoor Championships, the Diamond League final and the World Championships title. He capped off the year by receiving the Lonsdale Cup presented by the New Zealand Olympic Committee, given to a New Zealand athlete who has demonstrated the most outstanding contribution to an Olympic or Commonwealth sport during the previous year.

Hamish was incredibly humbled to be named the Supreme Award winner. “To be judged the best for last year was a very special moment.” His success was made all the better when his coach James Sandilands was named Coach of the Year, his first win in the category.

2026 has started off just as strong for Hamish, with a 2.28m season opener at Track Stars, a televised event as part of the four-day National Athletics Championships held in Auckland in March. “It is always fun to jump in front of a home crowd!”

It’s one thing to look at an issue and bemoan the fact that something should be done about it, and quite another to actually do something yourself. Richard Douglas took the latter route. As an Ear, Nose and Throat (ENT) surgeon at Auckland City Hospital, he is keen to see equitable access to primary health care so people can be referred for specialist care as needed.

Northland became his focus, with the identified need for a mobile ENT Clinic. After consultation with Whangārei otolaryngologist surgeon Dr David Waterhouse as to what a best-practice clinic on wheels might look like, Richard personally funded the design and build of the vehicle himself, as well as the hospital-level fit out.

Left: A young patient being examined. Top right: Tarāpunga, the mobile Ear, Nose and Throat (ENT) clinic. Lower right: Professor Richard Douglas (driving) and Dr David Waterhouse in the van on the way to a mobile medical clinic. Photo credit: Medical Assurance Society (MAS) / Medium Rare.

Tarāpunga (the red-billed seagull) is the name aptly gifted to the mobile van by Northland kaumātua. The gull is a familiar sight around the region’s coasts, just as the van is. The mobile ENT clinic, run by David, delivers specialist healthcare to harder-to-reach communities, as well as in Whangārei. With the service being in place for over a year, Richard is happy with the progress. “Now, regardless of location, people have access to essential medical services. The virtual clinic has access to someone’s health record online so David is also seeing people fully cognisant of their health history and can further add to it.”

Richard’s inspiration for mobile care comes from another Old Boy, Sir Patrick Eisdell Moore ‘30. As a medical officer with the 28th Māori Battalion in WW II, he learnt that the ear health of their children was poor. After the war he built an ‘ear caravan’ and towed it with his Land Rover to East Cape. His multiple referrals of children to Greenlane Hospital for grommets to fix their ‘glue ear’ were life-changing for them. Two good Grammar men, 45 years apart, making mobile ENT health a reality.

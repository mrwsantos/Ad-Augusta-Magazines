Anzac Day

They were staunch to the end, against odds uncounted,

They fell with their faces to the foe.

Auckland Grammar School held its 108th annual Anzac Day service in the Great Hall, where those in attendance heard fromDr John Etty as this year’s guest speaker.

Current students and parents, Old Boys, members of the New Zealand Defence Force, and the wider Grammar community assembled for the service as has been the tradition since 1918, two years after the Great Hall was built. With 10% of the country’s population at the time serving in World War I, the first service would have been most poignant. Many Old Boys and Masters had already perished, and it began an annual tradition that has continued for over 100 years.

This year’s student address was given by Senior Prefect Rupert Glaetzer, following a welcome from Headmaster Tim O’Connor and the Ecclesiasticus reading by Head Prefect Nico Stanley.

Form 5 student Harold MacCulloch was the drummer who led the wreath bearers to the War Memorial: Old Boy Sir Christopher Mace, KNZM ‘57 and his grandson Samuel on behalf of the Old Boys’ Association, with Old Boy Hon David Seymour ‘97 and Tibbs House Head Prefect Ryland Te Tamaki on behalf of the School.

A minute’s silence with the flag at half-mast was followed by a reading of the Ode, For the Fallen by R. L. Binyon, published in September 1914, read first in Te Reo Māori by Deputy Head Prefect Matayas Thomson, then in English by Senior Prefect Xavier Clark. The Last Post and Rouse performed on the trumpet by senior student Jonathan Chang from the C Floor was, as always, an emotional end to the service.

In his address, Dr Etty - who holds a PhD in History - discussed the enduring relationship between education, service, and sacrifice, using the history of Auckland Grammar School as its central lens.

Beginning with the reforms of educationalist George Hogben, Dr Etty emphasises the longstanding belief that “schools had a moral responsibility; a responsibility to develop in young people the habits and character that would shape their lives, but also the future of society.” For George Hogben and his contemporaries, this meant instilling values such as discipline, patriotism, and service, qualities that would later define a generation called to war. “This patriotic urge to enlist motivated volunteers all over the world. The excitement for war seemed odd in the years afterwards.”

Dr Etty’s address focused particularly on the experiences of Grammar masters and Old Boys during both World Wars. Teachers such as James Dinneen and Frederick Airey are remembered not only for their service in the classroom, but also for their leadership and sacrifice on the battlefield. Dr Etty noted that Dinneen left his teaching role to serve as a captain and died following the Battle of the Somme in 1916. “In the report on his death published in the School Chronicle, a private who served with James Dinneen described him as ‘strict and conscientious’, a ‘gallant officer” and someone who did more for his men than any other officer. His service to his country was matched by his service to his comrades.”

Drawing on examples from ancient societies, Dr Etty highlighted that education has historically combined intellectual and physical development to prepare individuals for leadership in all aspects of life. He referred to education theorist Gert Biesta: “Education has three purposes: the transmission of knowledge and skills, induction into existing cultural traditions, and the development of individuals capable of independent thought and responsible action.”

In a contemporary context, Dr Etty acknowledges that today’s students face a complex and uncertain world shaped by global challenges. As such, the role of education in fostering sound judgement, resilience, and a commitment to democratic values is more important than ever. He called upon the School communityto remember those who served.

“Teaching, like fighting, is an act of faith. Faith that the future remains open. Faith that it matters enough to commit a lifetime to. Faith that the formation of young people represents society’s best hope. That is how this school remembers these men - not only as soldiers, but also as teachers. We remember what they understood: that forming the next generation, and fighting for the next generation, are among the most serious things a person can do. That it was worth their lives’ work to do it well. And that manyof them gave their lives in the process.”

Studying A Level History, Senior Prefect Rupert Glaetzer’s Anzac address focused on Grammar’s impact on the war and the valiant and selfless sacrifice of those Grammar boys who fought in the World Wars. He spoke about the harrowing cost of World War I. “New Zealand sent more men to fight per head of population than any other nation, yet 18,166 souls never returned home, more than 500 of them dying from disease in training camps, before they evensaw the frontline of battle.”

He told those gathered about Sapper Richard Hobbs Beamish, a bright student who started at Grammar in 1908 in 3A. Richard was a sharp target shooter and after moving to England in 1912, he enlisted with the New Zealand Engineers, beginning basic trainingin Egypt. Sadly, he was sent home ill to New Zealand in 1916 and died a year later from tuberculosis in 1917, aged just 23 years old.

The names on the plaques surrounding the War Memorial of Old Boys who perished in World War II were added in 1953, honouring 400 Grammar men who gave their lives for their country. Among them was Sergeant Lauritz Andrew Woodney (Laurie) Rasmussen.

Rupert told of his schooling life before being called to serve. “Entering Auckland Grammar School in 1936, Laurie was just like us, a student with a fine academic record who spent his weekends playing club Cricket for Grafton and Rugby for the Grammar Old Boys. But when the world went to war, he traded the classroom for the cockpit. He became the youngest New Zealander to serve inthe Battle of Britain, tasked with protecting the skies over England.”

Sharing of Laurie’s “hauntingly brief” time as a soldier on the frontline was confronting. “Just 19 hours after being appointed an air gunner, Laurie climbed into his Defiant aircraft for his first mission, an interception patrol. Tragically, the plane never gained altitude, striking the ground shortly after takeoff. Laurie never got to finish that mission, He was a boy who left our classrooms to defend our freedom. He was just 18 years old.”

Rupert also spoke of his personal connection to Anzac Day. “My great-grandfather fought in World War II with the Royal Australian Air Force. So standing here today, I am reminded that Anzac Day has never belonged to one nation alone. The bond between Australia and New Zealand was forged from the trenches at Gallipoli to the skies in Britain. This was sustained through common sacrifice and generations of service.”

Rupert then drew attention to those Old Boys of Auckland Grammar School who never returned from war. “The men we remember today were students, teachers, sons, brothers, and friends. They all had futures that were full of possibility and dreams. Futures they willingly set aside for something greater than themselves. As students, we inherit not only a fine education, but a responsibility: to remember, to respect, and to uphold the values they stood for.”

Rupert concluded his address by emphasising the importance of remembering those who have gone before us: “Today, as we stand in silence, may we remember that the freedoms we enjoy were not free. They were hard earned, by ordinary people, people like us, people who showed extraordinary courage when the world and New Zealand needed it. Lest we forget.”

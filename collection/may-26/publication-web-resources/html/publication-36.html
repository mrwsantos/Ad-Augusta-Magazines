How, you might well think, does the School have anything to do with that symbol of Australian/English cricket rivalry, “The Ashes”.

The link comes through Samuel Percy Jones, who was born in Sydney, Australia, on 1 August 1861. From his early days at Sydney Grammar School, Jones showed considerable cricket ability as both a batsman and a bowler. He first came to public notice with his bowling performance in his school’s first return match against Melbourne Grammar, when he took 11 wickets for 45 runs over the two innings.

He made his test debut in 1881 against England in Australia and, not quite 21 years old, he toured England in the Australian team in 1882, when they defeated England for the first time on English soil at the Kennington Oval.

The weather was fine but the pitch was rather bumpy on the first day, and Australia was bowled out for only 63 runs in the first innings, Jones scoring a duck. One of the England openers was the famous W G Grace, who was bowled out for only 4 runs, and their innings closed at 101.

On the second day, despite showery conditions, Australia made 122 runs. Jones was run out for 6. At the end of this play, assuming the ball was dead, Jones left his crease to pat down the pitch and was promptly run out by W. G. Grace. The Australians were incensed at the unfairness of it (think ‘underarm bowling’ in more recent times!).

After the innings, the Australian fast bowler, Fred Spofforth, known as the ‘Demon Bowler’, poked his head around the door of the English dressing room and informed W G Grace that his gesture would “cost you the match”. Sure enough, he took 7 wickets for 44 runs and England were all out for 77, 8 runs short of a win.

The next day, the English journalist, Reginald Shirley Brooks of the Sporting Times, published a mock ‘obituary’.

“In Affectionate Remembrance of English Cricket, which died at the Oval on 29th August, 1882. Deeply lamented by a large circle of sorrowing friends and acquaintances R.I.P. N.B. - The body will be cremated and the ashes taken to Australia.”

An English team, captained by Ivo Bligh, toured Australia in late 1882, vowing to return the mythical “Ashes” to England. Having won the series, he was presented in Australia with a small urn, containing ashes, which may have been of a bail, a ball or even a lady’s shawl. On his death, the urn was presented to the Marylebone Cricket Club (MCC) at Lord’s in London and resides in the museum there. Many replicas of the urn have been made and used in subsequent test series as a trophy for the team which won or retained “the Ashes”.

Jones went on to tour England for Australia in 1886 (when he scored 1497 runs at 24.95 and two centuries, including 151 against the Gentlemen at The Oval), 1888 and 1890.

During the 1886 tour, he became seriously ill with smallpox (although originally stated as rheumatic fever) and an ambiguous message back to Australia resulted in his death being reported.

While on the 1890 tour, Jones was again hospitalised, this time with an abscess. Once able to travel, he was being rowed on an evening out to the sailing ship, Sabraon, in Plymouth Harbour bound for Australia, when he became lost and was afloat all night, not reaching the ship until first light.

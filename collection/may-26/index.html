Reflections

03Reflections by the Headmaster

“Grammar’s school culture permeates the School daily, starting with the full School assembly.” These few words were part of my first impressions of Auckland Grammar School, shared in my first Ad Augusta editorial in May 2013. Tim O’Connor, Headmaster

I raise this reflection, 13 years on, after contemplating not only what our young men achieved in Term 1, 2026, but also on a culture that has challenged them to succeed primarily in their studies, as well as in their extracurricular commitments and personal lives.

As you read this edition of Ad Augusta, you will not only see diversity of achievement, but you will read of the environment we aspire to create and maintain, without compromise.

My précis of Grammar in May 2026 is that it is a School with a clear educational philosophy and a defined culture that permeates it with deliberate intent and regularity, making it a high-purpose environment.

In The Culture Code, Daniel Coyle says, “successful groups communicate their purpose with the subtlety of a punch in the nose. They devote a surprising amount of time to reminding each other precisely what they stand for and repeating it, ad infinitum.”

Auckland Grammar School knows who it is. To some, our School is shrouded in mystery, while others think the successes Grammar boys have achieved over 157 years stem from privilege or a mystical drive from unique and talented young men. I would suggest otherwise.

It starts with assembly. It is the central point of our day, without exception. Our daily assembly reflects who we are. The ritual of ringing the handbell, the reading of the School Prayer, and the precision of our young men standing and being seated may be simple to some, but in a Grammar environment, these are beacons that focus everyone present. These beacons are signals that endorse our culture and reflect a high-purpose environment each day.

What follows is very clear messaging. Successes are to be celebrated. This is symbolised by ‘crossing of the stage’, which is earned and respected by the wider School and masters alike.

Successes celebrated in the year to date began with the naming of Nico Stanley as the 2026 Head Prefect, followed by the 2025 Scholars’ assembly with Taejeong Kim named the top scholar, through to the Premier Tennis team being named National Champions, and the Senior Eight Rowing crew earning Silver at the Maadi Cup in Twizel.

Our daily assembly is where our standards are clearly communicated and repeated. As Daniel Coyle says, “high-purpose environments are filled with small, vivid signals designed to create a link between the present moment and a future ideal. They provide two simple locators that every navigation process requires: Here is where we are, and Here is where we want to go.”

While abstract, the School’s educational philosophy supports where we are in 2026 and the style of education we will provide in the future. Specifically, it states, “the guardians of Auckland Grammar School have a responsibility to ensure that the minds of those in their care are trained with reason and vigour, while allowing their education to be strengthened by challenge and debate.”

At a time of considerable change in the New Zealand education system, our educational philosophy and the high-purpose nature of our environment should provide our community with reassurance. Change is not to be feared; it must first align with our philosophy and culture to be implemented at Auckland Grammar School.

The permeation of our culture is deliberate and planned. It supports a philosophy that is focused on the teaching of foundational content knowledge. However, a Grammar boy also knows, through assembly messaging and role modelling, that the standard he walks past is the standard he accepts in his life. This flows through to his understanding that learning can be difficult, but that if he embraces mistakes and challenges, he will learn from them rather than making excuses.

A Grammar boy knows he should be continually working to expand his belief in what he is capable of. But he has to accept the challenge himself, work in a self-disciplined fashion and use the environment he is part of to his advantage if he is to grow in anti-fragility and independence.

There is no mystical formula for individual or group success. It is about building a high-purpose environment where we share a steady stream of ultra-clear signals that align with our educational philosophy and our shared goal of having students regularly achieve personal bests.

Per Angusta Ad Augusta.
Tim O’Connor, Headmaster

04From the Board

"On behalf of the Board, I welcome all students for the 2026 academic year, not only the Form 3 cohort commencing their time at the School, but also those who are returning to continue their education. We look forward to your contributions across all aspects of curricular and extracurricular activities available at Grammar." Grant Trethewey, Board Chair

I would also like to welcome the wider Grammar community, particularly those parents embarking on their time at Grammar with their sons. Your support will help to ensure you enjoy the experience of being associated with Grammar, which strives to make opportunities available for your sons.

Once again, we have a very large Form 3 cohort, in excess of 500, which contributes to a total school roll of more than 2,800. With the continuing demand for places at the School and the Government and local authority focus on intensification in the School zone, the roll must be managed on a year-by-year basis, given that we are obliged to take all eligible students from within the Grammar zone.

The Board continues to review the effects of current enrolment legislation on our ability to operate the School at the high standards we strive for, whilst ensuring compliance with the requirements of health and safety standards to ensure the well-being of students, support staff and teachers. This is becoming increasingly difficult given the current and forecast growth for Auckland and the potential implications of the plans for intensification in the Grammar zone. We continue to discuss these issues with the Ministry of Education, both at an official and political level to ensure that the School can continue to maintain its long-standing values and quality of education.

In 2025, the School achieved outstanding academic results and, in a key indicator, had a University Entrance pass rate of 87%, maintaining the School’s primary goal of achieving academic excellence. The Board believes it is important to provide the best environment for our teachers and students to push these achievements to higher levels as the School continues to grow. To do this, we are endeavouring to build upon the excellent foundations already in place, both in terms of teaching facilities and the quality of teachers.

A major initiative in terms of maintaining and enhancing the School’s primary focus on academia, and the teachers required to support that focus, is the ongoing strategy of growing and managing the School campus to provide options for improved facilities for teachers and students, including the option of further housing for teachers.

To that end, the proposed purchase of 10 Clive Road and the ongoing funding to achieve that acquisition will provide the School with further space in the form of a 2,000 square metre property adjacent to Tibbs House. This acquisition will provide the School with additional options, including the ability to offer more housing to assist teachers to overcome one of the major constraints for teachers at Grammar.

The purchase of 10 Clive Road will require extensive support from the wider Grammar community. I would like to thank those who have already contributed and to encourage those in our Grammar community to also become involved in what is a long-term project to assist in ensuring the future successful growth of the School.

Per Angusta Ad Augusta.
Grant Trethewey ‘76 — Board Chair

05High Purpose and Deep Belonging

The intersection of identity and education

Professor Gert Biesta is a world-renowned educational philosopher and author whose work challenges schools to move beyond “learning” toward the cultivation of human character and responsibility. His educational philosophy explores how purpose and belonging empower students to become themselves — a philosophy embraced by Headmaster Tim O’Connor.

“Education is not just about the acquisition of knowledge; it is about the provocation of being in the world in a grown-up way. It is the beautiful risk of becoming a subject who acknowledges they are part of something larger than themselves.” Professor Gert Biesta

Left: Belonging in practice — a 1st XV player is engulfed by students on the sideline. Right: Headmaster Tim O’Connor congratulates Head Prefect Nico Stanley as he crosses the stage at the first assembly of the 2026 academic year.

Headmaster Tim O’Connor has a bookshelf packed with inspirational reading material. On there you will find the work of Professor Gert Biesta, whose educational philosophy is of great interest to him. “In the modern educational landscape, it is easy to get lost in ‘how we learn’ (the process), and Biesta prompts educators to reflect on ‘the what’ (content) and ‘why’ (purpose) that is needed to help create a high-purpose environment in which our students can thrive.”

Biesta suggests that we are in danger of turning education into a simple transaction where students are consumers and teachers are merely facilitators. He describes this as “learnification,” which he says is simply not good enough.

He argues that schools must balance academic results with the development of a student’s unique character within a high-purpose environment. Biesta says the environment must do three things simultaneously to succeed: Qualification, Socialisation, and Subjectification.

Qualification

A school of excellence must master qualification, which Tim notes has always been central to a Grammar education. “The School has the necessary academic rigour in place to provide students with the credentials to navigate the world.”

Socialisation

Tim says that Biesta points to the necessity of the environment to provide a profound sense of belonging. “With socialisation he talks about the need for students to be initiated into a specific tradition, a lineage of values, and a community that cares for their development, and this is something we see as absolutely key at Grammar.”

This ‘belonging’ is not about blind conformity; it is about providing the stable ground upon which a student can stand and feel part of a community they feel they can excel in.

Tim references another well-leafed through book in Owen Eastwood’s ‘Belonging’. “Eastwood tells us that deep belonging creates a profound sense of psychological safety and identity that allows someone to shift from survival mode into the higher-level cooperation and risk-taking required for elite performance. We want boys to feel a keen sense of belonging at Grammar.”

Subjectification

Subjectification, Biesta says, defines the soul of a high-purpose school. This is the process of a student becoming a ‘subject’ of their own life, rather than merely an ‘object’ of educational interventions — the learner or the consumer. They become more autonomous and independent in their thinking as individuals.

For Biesta, Tim says, “the ultimate purpose of an educational environment is to help individuals exist in the world in a ‘grown-up way.’ This doesn’t mean being an adult; it means existing in a way that acknowledges that they are not the centre of the universe.”

The Teacher as “Interrupter”

In a high-purpose environment, Biesta says the teacher is not just a ‘provider of learning opportunities.’ They are someone who provides high-quality input, frequent response opportunities, and rigorous feedback. In doing so the teacher ‘interrupts’ the student’s current way of being. This interruption is an act of care. It signals to the student: “I see you, I believe you are capable of more, and I am calling you into a higher way of existing.”

Tim encourages his staff to ‘interrupt their subjects.’ “When we focus on seeing every student, regardless of their stream, as a ‘subject’ in waiting, we create the conditions for excellence. It is through the quality of our relationships with them and the depth of our purpose that we can support their growth here at Grammar.”

A high-purpose educational environment is a deliberate architecture. It requires the ability to provide qualification, furnish a community to socialise in and belong to, and gives the opportunity for students to ‘grow up’ within this structure with some ‘intentional interruption’ from their teachers to help them excel.

Beyond the First Year

06 Beyond the First Year

A second year for learning a second language

Anyone who has started the journey of learning a second language will tell you it takes time. As of next year, Auckland Grammar School is going to give students more time to cement their learning in a second language, by making their language choice a compulsory two-year course rather than just a single year in Form 3.

Head of Faculty for Languages, Carlin Shaw is pleased the School has taken this decision. “Not all schools make a commitment to helping to build more balanced students by making learning a second language a part of everyday school life. Grammar has deemed this important to a young man’s learning and given them the opportunity to get a good grasp of their chosen language.”

1) Form 4 student Tautahi Stone works on his reo; 2) Latin Teacher Michael Dong and Form 4 student Sebastian Harvey discuss ‘beware of the dog’ in Latin; 3) Head of Department – Spanish Michelle McNeill talks food with her class. Opposite: Marie Gaspar with her Form 4 French students.

The five languages on offer remain as French, Japanese, Latin, Te Reo Māori and Spanish. In learning them, a student learns far more than how to read, write and recite a language that has hitherto been foreign to them. Carlin says it enables them to gain a different perspective on life. “Language is deeply intertwined with culture, and learning another tongue breaks down stereotypes and builds empathy. When a student studies Māori, for example, they gain insight into te ao Māori (the Māori world) and tikanga (customs), expanding their understanding of local identity. Likewise, Spanish and French provide windows into diverse global communities and histories, while Latin provides a basis for understanding European language, structures and thought. Japanese society and history are fascinating and their pop culture storytelling through ‘animanga’ (anime and manga) is well known.”

This exposure to a culture different from their own can help a student move away from a monocultural mindset, foster tolerance and gain a genuine appreciation for diversity. These are traits that are imperative in an increasingly interconnected world, particularly when you live in a borderless island nation, far from the countries that speak those languages.

“If you talk to a man in a language he understands, that goes to his head. If you talk to him in his language, that goes to his heart.”Nelson Mandela

Spending time mastering a second language, Carlin notes, can also have a useful effect on the other subjects a student is taking. “It engages students in critical thinking, which can strengthen their performance across the board.” Studies have shown positive correlations between second language learning and academic achievement. Research also shows that being bilingual can lead to improved listening skills, since the brain has to work harder to distinguish different types of sounds in two or more languages. Furthermore, a growing awareness of the abstract rules and structure of a second language can also make someone more cognisant of the rules around their first language.

Multiple studies over time have concluded that learning a new language makes a difference to the way your brain develops and functions. The better you learn, the more those vital areas of the brain grow. While this is well documented in children who grow up in a bilingual environment, there are still proven neurological benefits for adults of any age learning a second language.

Having new experiences is an important factor in forming new connections in the brain. The novelty of a new task, combined with the repetition of practice, is one of the reasons that language learning is such an effective brain workout. For older adults it is considered a protective factor against dementia and other degenerative neurological conditions.

As a young person entering a competitive job market, Carlin suggests having some knowledge of a second language and its culture is an advantage. “In a globalised economy, employers are increasingly seeking staff who can bridge cultural and linguistic divides. Learning another language prepares students for international opportunities, whether in business, diplomacy, or travel. In a local context, learning Māori helps build a stronger, more inclusive community by fostering respectful, cross-cultural connections.”

By offering a two-year course in their chosen language, Grammar students will have the opportunity to engage with both living cultures and foundational linguistic structures for a longer period. Carlin believes the class of 2027 and beyond will benefit from this change. “I think it is a move that will be good for our young men on so many levels.”

Character Education

07 Character Education

Becoming a Good Grammar Man

At Auckland Grammar School, ‘The Grammar Way’ is at the very heart of the School’s ethos. Often quoted, it is not just a saying, it is a way of being.

By weaving Grammar’s seven core values of integrity, service, respect, resilience, humility, commitment, and courage into the fabric of daily life at the School, Grammar provides a moral compass for the students in our care.

The nurturing and demonstration of the collective standard that encompasses ‘The Grammar Way’ is there to help transform teenage boys into cognisant, principled and determined young men. As they enter adulthood, we want Grammar men to embrace challenges and uphold the values of a good man in all aspects of his life.

1) Form 7 student Lachlan Cameron helps a Form 3 student on the first day of school in 2026; 2) A group of Duke of Edinburgh Award boys enjoy time together at Piha on their Bronze Practice Journey. Opposite: 2025 Rope Cup recipient James Hiddleston ‘21 accepting the cup from Government Minister Hon. Paul Goldsmith ‘84 at the 2025 Prizegiving.

An education at Auckland Grammar School provides more than formal qualifications. It should furnish a young man with an education in good character.

In his inaugural report in 1954, the School’s eighth headmaster, Sir Henry Cooper, identified that “the School seeks to graduate boys with valuable gifts of temperament and character...and men of quality.” His wish was for them to “have developed a healthy attitude to life...and it is on their efficient work and loyalty and their appreciation of their obligations as citizens that the well-being of the country depends.”

Over 60 years later, this vision still stands, albeit in a 2020s context. Sir Henry Cooper’s aspiration has since had much thought and structure put around it, which eventuated in ‘The Grammar Way.’

Auckland Grammar School is anchored by seven core values that form the foundation of ‘the Grammar Way.’ These values are designed to guide students’ behaviour and character development.

The School integrates these seven values into daily life, from assembly addresses by the Headmaster and teachers’ expectations in the classroom, to knowing what conduct is expected on the sports field or sideline and at any time a student is seen in uniform outside the school gates. The goal is to graduate young men of good character.

Becoming a good man, through the lens of ‘The Grammar Way’ focuses on these seven values and their “student-speak outcomes”, noted below:

Integrity

Demonstrating integrity means having strong moral principles and acting in accordance with them. “We support our mates and do what is right, even when no one is looking.”

Excellence

Excellence comes from a determination to acquire knowledge and skills of the highest standard. “We put in the hard yards and keep our standards high.”

Respect

Respect means having due regard for the feelings, rights and wishes of others. ”We treat others how we want to be treated and give respect to receive respect.“

Courage

It takes courage to face challenges and discomfort. “We stand up and stand strong for what we believe in.”

Pride

Pride means having satisfaction in our achievements. “We are not afraid to be ourselves and are proud to wear the Lion.”

Commitment

Commitment means being dedicated to a cause. “We live up to our promises, and we never give up.”

Humility

Someone who demonstrates humility has a modest view of their own importance. It involves acknowledging the contributions of others and being a ‘quiet achiever.’ “We are the ‘bigger man’ and are willing to learn from others.”

If living by ‘The Grammar Way’, a Grammar man can answer his question of, “Who am I?” He is a man with a sense of whakapapa. He understands his identity and his heritage and knows the values that shape him. He takes responsibility for his actions and strives for personal growth.

If he asks himself, “Where do I fit in and how can I best serve others?” he can answer that question too. He has a sense of tūrangawaewae, and understands his place in the world. He embodies whanaungatanga, contributing positively to those around him and makes a lasting impact. He leads with humility, service and integrity. He is a good man.

Growing the Grammar Precinct

08 Growing the Grammar Precinct

Why 10 Clive Road is an important legacy project for the Grammar community

Every so often an opportunity arises that simply can’t be missed. The chance to buy 10 Clive Road is one such moment. Situated directly adjacent to existing school-owned land, No. 10 offers a rare chance for Auckland Grammar School to expand its presence along Clive Road.

The legacy this piece of land will provide is the ability to future-proof the campus by providing an opportunity to grow the Grammar precinct. This strategic purchase for the School needs the wider Grammar community’s support to make it happen. Once secured, the possibilities are exciting. Headmaster Tim O’Connor sets out his vision for what that could be.

10 Clive Road is a 2000m² site that runs between Clive Road and Castle Drive. It adjoins 8 Clive Rd, already owned by the School. With No.8 neighbouring Tibbs House (the School’s boarding establishment), the addition of No.10 provides a contiguous land holding.

Neighbours who are neighbourly often talk over the fence and catch up with each other’s lives. It was over one such neighbourly chat in late 2024 that Headmaster Tim O’Connor heard from Norma Willis that she was thinking about selling her home of 54 years at 10 Clive Road and wondered whether Grammar might like to be the new owner.

It was not a spur of the moment thought. It was something Norma had thought long and hard about and concluded it would be the best outcome. “I wanted Grammar to have it. I have always had a great admiration for the School and it seemed like a very logical thing to me, for this property to become part of Grammar.”

Funding the purchase

Tim’s reaction to Norma’s proposition was a positive one; he just needed to figure out how to make it happen. As is the way at Grammar, there are always wise heads available to consult about possibilities, and there is always a way forward. ‘No’ is simply not an option.

The solution to taking up the opportunity came about with a generous offer from an Old Boy to pay the $1.5 million deposit. Another Old Boy offered to pay the balance of the purchase price to purchase the property himself in the interim, so that Norma could move out and move on with her life. The generosity of these two men ensured the property did not go to the open market but was instead secured.

It also gave Grammar time to raise the funds to pay back the temporary owner of the house. If the purchase price can be paid back in full by the end of 2026, the property will be transferred into a trust entity at Auckland Grammar School.

Jeff Blackburn, Director of Advancement, has been pleased with the fundraising support to date to ensure the house comes into the School’s possession. “The Grammar community understands this is an opportunity that cannot be missed, and to that end a handful of generous donors have enabled us to reach the $1 million repayment milestone we needed to meet at the end of 2025.”

However, 2026 he says is another year, with the remaining fundraising target to reach, to guarantee that the School can own the property, come year-end. “The figure we need to raise this year is almost double what we raised last year, at $1.91 million.” With just over $600,000 raised towards that goal in the first three months of the year, the momentum needs to continue to find the remaining $1.3 million by December 2026.

Headmaster Tim O’Connor acknowledges the enduring commitment of the Grammar community to actively make the campus their own, decade after decade. “As we have done in the past, with the impact of motorways, pools that are no longer fit for purpose, libraries, theatres, pavilions and the like, we take control of our own destiny through the active engagement of our community, feeding forward to provide us with the Auckland Grammar School we have today. It is now our turn as we look ahead to provide future students, staff, headmasters and boards with the legacy purchase of 10 Clive Road, and the enormous amenity that will bring.”

On a summery evening in March, in the ample back yard of No. 10, Headmaster Tim O’Connor took the chance to outline his vision for the addition of the property to the campus to assembled guests. “Job one, is of course to secure 10 Clive Road, so it doesn’t go back out onto the open market. If we don’t get it, it’s gone and we’ll never get another opportunity to purchase this strategically positioned piece of land.” Given that unthinkable outcome, he encouraged the audience to not only actively consider donating to the cause themselves, but to champion it to others in our community.

Lead Feature

The vision of a Grammar precinct

On a March evening in 2026, 10 Clive Road was lit up with the School’s colours of blue and gold to discuss the vision to purchase the property for Auckland Grammar School. Left: Headmaster Tim O’Connor at the gate (left) with previous owner Norma Willis and Board Chair Grant Trethewey

“Job two”, Tim went on to explain, “is to develop the detailed vision — an aspiration for a new Grammar precinct. That is, the dream use of Tibbs House land and buildings, 8 Clive Rd land and residence and its neighbour 10 Clive Road.” Between them, No. 8 and No.10 equate to 3000 square metres of land, a sizeable portion of land in central Auckland. The proximity of Tibbs House provides possibilities when the three properties are viewed collectively.

Tim describes ‘a Grammar precinct’ as the most apt terminology for the amenity that 10 Clive Road would add. “Going forward, roll size or demand for places at Grammar will not diminish. Our roll is at its largest in our history, at almost 2800. This year we have a cohort of 564 in Form 3, a 5th Form of 608 students and a 7th Form with 490. We are in unprecedented times.” The growing roll means more teachers, more students and more boarders.

Staff housing

Staff wanting to live in the Grammar zone to ensure they are within a reasonable commute to work are committing to high housing costs, which Tim spells out is challenging on a teacher’s salary. “Going forward, I can’t see that the cost of living in the Grammar Zone will decrease. Living in Auckland outside the zone is also expensive, and there is no ‘Auckland allowance’ for a teacher — they are paid the same as a teacher in Invercargill.”

While the School’s charitable entity in the Foundation Trust offers some extra financial reward through the (competitive) Staff Awards at year-end and a modest donation towards the time spent in extra-curricular support of students, it is the possibility of subsidised housing that is hugely helpful to Grammar teachers.

Tim hopes that a Grammar precinct could provide townhouses for teachers at the 10 Clive Road site and through the back of Barnett Crescent, creating much more staff housing in the available footprint of land owned by the School.

The need is evident with an ever-growing waiting list for the 17 rent-subsidised staff houses on Barnett Crescent and along the northern side of Clive Road. Appropriate housing is not only an excellent way to attract and retain good teachers to teach at Grammar. Their proximity to campus enables them to be closely involved in the day-to-day life of the School.

Tim paints the picture of what a typical morning looks like on the Grammar campus with staff involvement in activities well before the assembly bell rings. “In this past week, if you walked across the campus, before 7am you would have seen the likes of Mr Adam and Mr Trott leading cricket practices, and Dr Etty, Ms Greenslade, Ms Gaspar and Mr Lowe leading the ‘Learn to Row’ programme. You would have seen Mr Askew and Mr Rainger running members of the 1st XV while Mr Du Sart had others in the Weights Room, and you would have seen Mr Oates preparing boys for the Auckland Championships. That’s all before you arrive at Te Ara Mātauranga or onto the B Floor for Maths tuition.” Tim strongly feels that having more staff housing will be a fundamental requirement for the School going forward. “If we are to retain the essence of Grammar, we need more passionate teachers living close by, so their job is achievable and rewarding.”

Tibbs House

A Grammar precinct also involves the development of boarding. The current capacity for Tibbs House is 124 boys, but as Tim muses, “What is the ideal number for Tibbs? What if we could develop two separate boarding establishments on campus, each with 90 students, and they share a common dining hall?”

Then there are the questions about how best to prepare Form 7 boarders for the next phase of their lives. “What if we could provide our Form 7 boarders with flat-style accommodation so we can prepare them even better for independent living? What if we could have each of our boarders leave knowing how to cook five basic meals?”

Providing better spaces and amenities for boarders is not the only item on the wish list for Tibbs House. The accommodation for boarding masters needs to be addressed in any expansion plans. The current accommodation has a maximum of two bedrooms which precludes boarding masters with growing families. Having families live at Tibbs House provides a different feel to other boarding establishments at other schools and this is to be encouraged. “What does a new precinct look like where teachers and their families are part of a wider boarding community? While Auckland Grammar School is a school with boarding, rather than a boarding school, could we find some middle ground?” Tim muses.

A legacy that leads the way

Whatever the precise use of 10 Clive Road eventually is, it is the wider use of the various land holdings — either contiguous or proximal — that is important. Grammar has always strived for excellence in any endeavour, and, as Tim reflects, the 10 Clive Road legacy project is no different. “Why could we not lead the way? Why would we not create a Grammar precinct that attracts engaged and committed students and skilled, passionate practitioners (and their families) who are truly rewarded for the surplus value they provide to generations of Grammar boys? No. 10 symbolises our aspirations for the future of Grammar. If we are to protect the essence of a Grammar education in 10, 20 and 50 years’ time, we need to begin accepting responsibility for this now.”

The concept of ‘feeding forward’ is not new to the Auckland Grammar School community. For over 150 years the community of the day has supported the School to flourish not just for the time period they are in, but for future Grammar communities. Tim’s timeline to the assembled crowd on the back lawn at No.10 encompassed a sizeable list of changes to the campus, which have often been supported financially by the community.

“From the act of moving from Symonds Street to Mountain Road, the international architecture competition to design our main building, to the donations that made the war memorial a reality at the front of the School campus, being extended to the corner of Mountain Road and Clive Road to introduce boarding, the land exchange in 1963 that allowed the development of the motorway and the introduction of the lower fields and of course the purchase of corrections housing in 1994 that introduced the provision of teacher housing, followed most recently by the donation of the tennis courts in Clive Road — this all amounts to Grammar being an exceptional community asset.”

The focus now turns to securing 10 Clive Road. This key property will provide opportunities to anchor Tim’s vision for a growing Grammar precinct in the neighbourhood. “Grammar has enjoyed a strong presence in Mountain Road and Clive Road since 1916, and that presence will not diminish. Instead, through a Grammar precinct, it will become stronger.”

An Overview of the 2025 Academic Results

The 2025 academic results were very pleasing, with senior students performing extremely well in all external assessments.

The Cambridge examination performances, particularly for A Level, were very strong. The percentage of A* and A grades were 41.4%, with the overall pass rate sitting at 98.8%. The AS results were also fantastic, with the A-C pass rate exceeding almost 69% and the overall pass rate at 95.8%, the most successful pass rate for the past eight years. The School's NCEA cohort also performed very well. At NCEA Level 2, 69% of students gained their certificates, with 25 students gaining their certificates endorsed with Merit. At NCEA Level 3, 83% of students achieved the qualification, and 19 students had their certificates endorsed with Merit. Over 645 of NCEA Level 3 students achieved University Entrance in 2025.

Auckland Grammar School students study predominantly rigorous, academic subjects and standards which lead to comparatively high University Entrance rates for the Form 7 cohort. Cambridge students achieved a University Entrance pass rate of 99.6% in 2025 (up 0.3% from 2024), and the NCEA Form 7 cohort achieved 64.4% (up almost 5% year-on-year). Combined, the Form 7 students in 2025 achieved a University Entrance pass rate of 85.7%.

In 2025, the national pass rate for all students was 49.9% and the figure for all students in the School's Equity Index (EQI) was 70.1%. The 2025 national average for boys in schools in the EQI achieving University Entrance was 62.5%. Across all schools in New Zealand, the University Entrance rate for boys sat at 43.4%.

Auckland Grammar School was once again one of the top three performing schools in the country in NZQA Scholarship examinations, after students earned 198 individual scholarship awards in 2025. 122025 Scholars' Assembly

Minister of Education Hon. Erica Stanford spoke at the occasion.

At the 2025 Scholars' Assembly in late February, the Minister of Education, Hon Erica Stanford, was the guest speaker, addressing the 31 students who would soon cross the stage. "It is an incredible feat what the young men who will cross the stage today have achieved, and it is not to be underestimated the incredible amount of hard work, of dedication, of study, of sacrifice, and commitment that went into achieving the results we are here to celebrate today."

The Minister was clear in her recognition of what underpinned that achievement. "It is not just last year that we are celebrating, but the years that led up to that result." From primary school to intermediate through to their time at Auckland Grammar School, the Scholars' success reflects a long-term investment in learning, supported by teachers and families alike.

The Minister spoke of the privilege of an education at Auckland Grammar School. "You are extraordinarily lucky to be at a school like this, that instils in you some incredible values and really presses upon you the importance of academic achievement." She also stressed the importance of the responsibility that comes with it. "There is no greater gift than an amazing education," she said, describing it as something that can "change the trajectory of your life" and open doors that would otherwise remain closed. She also emphasised that it is not qualifications alone that matter. The habits formed along the way — focus, drive, determination, and a commitment to excellence — are what ultimately sets these young men apart.

"Not everybody has that," she observed. "The fact that you do will set you apart from everybody else." Her message was simple: take those attributes and apply them to everything. Even the most modest beginnings, she explained, can lead to unexpected opportunities.

The Minister then spoke about her first role right after graduation from university — she joked that her job was to make coffee and photocopies, "but I made the best coffee and I photocopied the best photocopying." That growth mindset, she explained, led to rapid progression, not because of the tasks themselves, but because of the attitude behind them.

Her advice to the Scholars was to carry that same approach into every endeavour. "Always give 120%," she said. "It will be noticed, and there will be more opportunities that open up for you in life outside of Grammar."

She also addressed the inevitability of setbacks. "I can tell you that along the way, you will fail," she said plainly. "But failure is not the problem. The problem is how you deal with failure, how you pick yourself up, you remember the lesson that you learned and you move forward." What matters, she explained, is resilience — the ability to learn, recover, and move forward with purpose.

Looking beyond the School, the Minister spoke about her wider ambitions for education in New Zealand. Her goal, she explained, is to ensure that every young person has access to the kind of opportunity represented by Auckland Grammar School — a system defined by "high expectations, high aspirations, and high goals." She pointed to ongoing work to strengthen the curriculum, invest in teachers, and raise the standard of national qualifications.

Central to that vision is a belief in equity. She quoted a phrase she heard from the Minister of Education in Singapore, who shared a principle that underpins her approach: "Your means should not determine your destiny." It is, she suggested, an ideal New Zealand must continue striving towards.

She concluded by challenging the Scholars to carry forward what they have gained. "Take what you have learned here," she said, "and make sure you carry that with you in whatever it is that you choose to do." Just as importantly, she encouraged them to remain connected to the School and, in time, to give back — to help future generations understand what it takes to succeed.

The Minister also acknowledged the role of parents, thanking them for the part they have played in shaping the young men celebrated on the day. Their contribution, she noted, is "so very crucial" to both character and achievement.

For those in attendance, the message was both one of recognition and of challenge. Their success deserves to be celebrated, but it is also a starting point. As the Minister reminded them, "you should be incredibly proud of yourselves" — and ready for what comes next.

After 13 years at Auckland Grammar School, Associate Headmaster – Academic, Dr John Etty is leaving to take on the role of Academic Director at The Teachers' Institute (TTI).

TTI is a provider of initial teacher education, so although John's departure is a loss to Grammar, his expertise is of huge advantage to the wider education sector in New Zealand. A good teacher has a profound influence on a young person and is borne out in the research, John says. John started at Grammar as a History teacher in 2012, and has also been Head of History, Head of Social Sciences, and Director of Teaching and Learning in his time at Mountain Road. Artist's Exhibition

Auckland Grammar School's very own 'artist in residence', Art master Justin Boroughs, had a solo exhibition of his work last November at Johnathan Grant Gallery in Auckland — his 26th solo exhibition. Justin's pieces are in public and private collections in New Zealand, most notably the Christchurch City Art Gallery, and around the world.

Justin was a student at the Elam School of Fine Arts in Auckland in the 1970s, studying under Gretchen Albrecht, Don Binney and John Turner. After travelling and painting extensively through Europe he returned to New Zealand in 1981, where he began a dual career as a painter and an art teacher at Grammar.

You could take a photograph there and save a lot of time and trouble. But a photograph never, ever, ever gives you a true idea of the landscape."

Faculty Profile

Excellence in Science Education

Where curiosity, rigor and discovery shape understanding.

Left: Head of Chemistry Sunit Keshwara observing senior students in his Chemistry class. Right: learning about motion in a Pre-Q Physics class.

Barbara McGowan, Head of Faculty Science, answers some questions about a faculty she is hugely passionate about.

Firstly, what is your personal background with science?

I completed a BSc in Molecular Biology and Genetics and a BA (Hons) in Sociology at Victoria University of Wellington. The best thing about a conjoint degree programme is the range of papers you get to take across multiple faculties — I was exposed to different ways of approaching knowledge and gathering information to form conclusions.

I then did a teaching diploma at Auckland College of Education, focusing on Science and Biology. Here at Auckland Grammar School, I mostly teach Biology, Junior Science and Earth and Space Science. I have also taught Chemistry at AS level and Pre-Q Physics — learning chemistry and physics again has reminded me of what it's like to be a student and improved the way I teach my core subjects.

Are other science teachers as passionate as you?

Yes! The Science faculty is amazing to work with. It is unusual to get to work with so many people who are passionate about science and encouraging students to study it at a higher level. The faculty is large; we have 33 full-time science teachers, two teachers who teach science and another subject, two teacher trainees, and five technical staff. Science is strong here — it is almost like teaching in a science academy!

What is the Science pathway and what do students learn?

All students at Grammar study Science in Forms 3 and 4, giving them a strong foundation for senior science study. In Form 5, there are a range of science options, as well as Triple Science, where students take Biology, Chemistry and Physics in two option lines so they can take an extra subject at Pre-Q. In Forms 6 and 7, students can choose Biology, Chemistry and Physics in the Cambridge and NCEA pathways; NCEA students can also study Earth and Space Science. The most capable students take scholarship examinations, and join Olympiad teams and the NZYPT (New Zealand Young Physicists' Tournament).

Biology — the living world

Biology explores the diversity of life and how organisms interact with their environments. Students study life processes, evolution, and ecology, with a special emphasis on our unique ecosystems, learning to make informed decisions around sustainability and biological issues.

Head of Faculty for Science — Barbara McGowan working with her Biology students.

Chemistry — the material world

Chemistry investigates the composition of matter and the energy involved in its transformations. Students learn to interpret the world through the behaviour of atoms, molecules, and ions, using scientific symbols and conventions — knowledge that helps them tackle global challenges like environmental sustainability and the development of new medicines and materials.

Physics — the physical world

This strand explains phenomena such as light, sound, electricity, and motion through the central concept of energy transformation, helping students engage with contemporary technological solutions and scientific challenges.

Earth and Space Science — planet Earth and beyond

Students explore the interconnecting systems of the Earth and the wider universe. By studying how the geosphere, hydrosphere, atmosphere, and biosphere depend on one another, students realise our role as guardians of finite resources.

How well do Grammar students do?

Over 90% of students choose to continue with at least one science in Form 5. In the 2025 Cambridge pathway for A Levels, an A–C grade was gained by 85% of Chemistry students, 83% of Physics students and 77% of Biology students studying A2. NCEA Level 3 achievement standards were gained by 78% (Biology), 77% (Chemistry), 64% (Earth and Space) and 58% (Physics) of students. Scholarships were awarded to 27 (Chemistry), 22 (Physics), 13 (Biology) and two (Earth and Space) students.

Why study the sciences?

The sciences encourage you to look at the world around you and question how it works — to look at things more closely and to step back and see human endeavours occurring within a much broader lens of how the living and non-living world interact. Given the increasing role of science in addressing big issues like climate change, it's important that everyone has a strong foundation in scientific concepts so they can make informed decisions.

Early in Term 1, over 200 guests attended the inaugural Lunar New Year to celebrate the Year of the Horse, a year traditionally associated with momentum, purpose, and forward movement.

With a resounding set from the Korean Nanta Drumming Group and the inaugural performance from the Auckland Grammar School Chinese Parent's Choir, the evening was one of celebration and renewed hope for the year ahead.

With guests gathered on site at 10 Clive Road, Headmaster Tim O'Connor made mention of the School's fundraising efforts to secure the property, but that the event was about coming together as one. "We respect the Year of the Horse because it has numerous links to who we are and what we aspire to achieve for the young men in our care. It is a symbol of energy, of resilience and determination in Lunar New Year traditions. The Horse represents progress, and the courage to move forward - qualities that I hope we encourage in your sons on a daily basis."

Old Boy and past parent Nelson Wang '90, who sits on the School Board, was in awe of the turnout from the inaugural event. "Wow, a sold-out event! Not bad for the first Lunar New Year celebration; we have set the bar pretty high for next year!"

An Asian migrant whose son Matthew finished his tenure at Grammar at the end of 2025, Nelson was very proud of the event. "We are a school with over 150 years of history, and tonight, you are making history, marking a new chapter in the Auckland Grammar School story." He was also heartened by the way the Asian culture and traditions were valued, and how the teaching staff helped inspire and motivate their sons each and every day.

"We know Auckland Grammar School is a very good school, but in order to help our boys achieve their best, it is not easy. It needs dedicated teachers, solid leadership and ongoing support from parents, so the fact that you have shown up tonight, means a lot and it makes Grammar stronger."

Boat Naming Ceremony

The rowing fraternity has six new boats.

Naming a rowing boat is not a regular occurence at the Auckland Grammar School Rowing Club. The last time everyone turned out to christen a new boat was in 2020 when the flagship Hudson Eight was named after long-time school and Club benefactor Peter Thompson. In prior years headmasters John Morris and Sir John Graham were similarly honoured, alongside Shiela, Lady Graham.

Co-Presidents of the Auckland Grammar School Rowing Club John Worth and Nikki Dines presided over the naming ceremony in late February for six new boats - three eights, two fours and a pair. Finding a name, John says, follows a convention. "The Club considers notable staff members and significant contributors to the School, remembrance, Olympic rowers and significant parent, staff or committee contributors when considering the name." Five of the new boats were named after Old Boys and one after a former master, with the rationale noted in the naming ceremony.

Harry Ashby. Harry '80 commenced his coaching after his son, Jackson '19, finished his time in the Grammar rowing program in 2023. Nikki reflects that he is adored by generations of novice rowers who appreciate "his balance of incredible coaching and ability to make anyone laugh during the tough moments."

Des Cutler II. Rowing was first established at Grammar in 1952 by Des. He began by coordinating with West End Rowing Club for a group of students who wanted to try the sport, and after decades of hard work and dedication, he built the club into a well-established institution. He was a staff member for 43 years from 1969 until his retirement in 2013, and Master in Charge of Rowing from 1970 to the mid 1990's.

Zac Gravatt II. Zac '01 was awarded Most Dedicated Rower for three consecutive years in his time as a Grammar Rower. It is an achievement rarely matched. The Sir John Buchanan Scholarship

Providing the opportunity of a Grammar education.

Lady Rosemary Buchanan lives in London, but every year in April she comes to New Zealand for a morning tea at Tibbs House to meet "my newest Tibbs House boy." Form 3 student Faifailemu Ah-Tuna has just started his Grammar journey as a Tibbs House boarder and is doing well. He is the 2025 recipient of the Sir John Buchanan Scholarship. This scholarship gives Faifailemu a full five-year boarding scholarship and it is named after Lady Buchanan's late husband, Sir John, who passed away in 2015.

Sir John Buchanan '56 set up the scholarship in 2013 in recognition of the role Auckland Grammar School played in his life. He felt his attendance at Grammar set him up well and wanted to give other boys the opportunity to have a Grammar education. The scholarship is awarded to a Year 8 student who preferably lives in South Auckland where John grew up. Rosemary also lived in South Auckland and says that the area's lack of proximity to Auckland Grammar School was part of Sir John's desire to get the scholarship underway. "John lived in Papatoetoe and used to get the train into Newmarket every day to attend Grammar. He said to me that the School 'gave me the best break I ever had' and as such he wanted other out-of-zone boys to have the same opportunity."

After they married in New Zealand, Rosemary and John travelled to the UK so John could undertake post-doctoral studies at the University of Oxford. "Our honeymoon was the boat trip over there." The UK became their home, and John made his way in the business world where he excelled. Sir John Buchanan was knighted in the 2012 New Year Honours for his extensive services to industry. He was recognised for his leadership as Chairman of Smith & Nephew and his previous high-level executive roles at BP.

While Sir John was alive, he took an active role in looking for potential leaders and role models to take up the scholarship and was thrilled to meet his first two boys who took up the opportunity in 2014 and 2015. After his death, Lady Buchanan and Sir John's brother Richard Buchanan '61 and wife Adele, have willingly continued Sir John's keenness to meet the scholarship awardees, and follow their journey through Grammar and beyond.

Lady Buchanan was thrilled to see that two previous scholarship recipients also attended the morning tea. Faleseu Fepuleai '14 is a Training Intern at Auckland City Hospital and arrived in his medical attire while on call. He was pleased he could get there. "I love coming back to Grammar to see Lady Buchanan if I can, and luckily for me I work close by, so I could make it happen." The two talked about his life post-school much to Lady Buchanan's delight. Makea Upu '20, the 2024 Tibbs House Head Prefect, is carving out a successful acting career on both sides of the Tasman. He arrived with a guitar and sang a poignant rendition of Stevie Wonder's Isn't She Lovely, which was an emotive moment for all attendees.

If he were still here, Sir John Buchanan would no doubt have felt his scholarship was serving the exact purpose he intended it for.

Farewell Shiela, Lady Graham

The longtime supporter of Grammar has passed away.

1) Shiela, Lady Graham and Sir John Graham in 2015; 2) Shiela presenting the Sir John Graham Scholarship Award to Frank Haszard '93 at the 2023 School Prizegiving; 3) The flag was flown at half-mast at Auckland Grammar School on Monday 20 April to acknowledge the passing of Shiela, Lady Graham.

On Monday 20 April, on the first day of Term 2, students walked in through the gates of Auckland Grammar School to see the flag flying at half-mast on the Main Block. In assembly that morning, Headmaster Tim O'Connor explained the reason for this rare occurrence was to mark the passing of Shiela, Lady Graham, the day before.

Lady Graham, aged 91, was the wife of the ninth headmaster of the School, Sir John Graham. His tenure ran for 20 years from 1973 to 1993 with Shiela by his side. Tim says her steadfast connection to the School has been exemplary. "Lady Graham was a stalwart supporter of Auckland Grammar School. Her long association with the School started when her husband, Sir John Graham was appointed, but she was a contributor to the School's culture in her own right. Shiela served as one of the School's first English as a Second Language teachers for over two decades."

When John was headmaster, the couple and their three children lived directly across the road at 52 Mountain Road. Integrally involved in all aspects of the School, events at Grammar were in many ways an extension of family life. The proximity meant it was just a few steps for their son Stuart Graham '80 to get to school, and a short walk for daughters Janet and Jennifer to attend Epsom Girls' Grammar School. While John was headmaster other family members also went through the School in nephews Grant Graham '75 and Stuart Graham '77.

Work and family coalesced at Grammar for Shiela and the connection did not stop once her husband left the post of headmaster in 1993. Grandsons Fergus Connolly '12 and Ethan Graham '16 also attended Grammar, providing personal reasons to stay connected.

After her husband passed away in 2017, Shiela continued to regularly attend events at the School. Tim says she enjoyed the continued association with the Grammar community and the School valued her willingness to stay connected. "We were most grateful for Lady Graham's connection and continued contributions to Auckland Grammar School. She remained involved with the life of the School and was genuinely interested in its standards being maintained for the benefit of all Grammar boys. As a defender of academic standards, Shiela was equally protective of the place of competition, not only in sport but as an integral part of developing a meritocratic school environment for boys. Her regular attendance at prizegivings, events, and services such as the ANZAC Remembrance service reinforced Shiela's strong connections with Auckland Grammar School."

In 2024, Shiela was named an Honorary Old Boy at the annual Augusta Awards in recognition of her ongoing support of Auckland Grammar School. It was a moniker she quietly relished after so many years of involvement at the School. Her presence will undoubtedly be missed at Auckland Grammar School. Vale Shiela, Lady Graham.

Anzac Day

Anzac Day

"They were staunch to the end, against odds uncounted, they fell with their faces to the foe."

1) Guests lay their poppies at the foot of the War Memorial after the service, with the 709 crosses on the front lawn; 2) Drummer Harold MacCulloch leads wreath bearers Sir Christopher Mace, KNZM '57 (left), his grandson Samuel, Tibbs House Head Prefect Ryland Te Tamaki and Hon David Seymour '97 to the War Memorial; 3) Academic Director of The Teachers' Institute Dr John Etty was this year's guest speaker; 4) Old Boy Ross Blair '55 with his medals; 5) It was lovely to see so many Old Boys in service uniform; 6) Members of the School's Symphony Orchestra.

Auckland Grammar School held its 108th annual Anzac Day service in the Great Hall, where those in attendance heard from Dr John Etty as this year's guest speaker.

Current students and parents, Old Boys, members of the New Zealand Defence Force, and the wider Grammar community assembled for the service as has been the tradition since 1918, two years after the Great Hall was built. With 10% of the country's population at the time serving in World War I, the first service would have been most poignant. Many Old Boys and Masters had already perished, and it began an annual tradition that has continued for over 100 years.

This year's student address was given by Senior Prefect Rupert Glaetzer, following a welcome from Headmaster Tim O'Connor and the Ecclesiasticus reading by Head Prefect Nico Stanley.

Form 5 student Harold MacCulloch was the drummer who led the wreath bearers to the War Memorial: Old Boy Sir Christopher Mace, KNZM '57 and his grandson Samuel on behalf of the Old Boys' Association, with Old Boy Hon David Seymour '97 and Tibbs House Head Prefect Ryland Te Tamaki on behalf of the School.

A minute's silence with the flag at half-mast was followed by a reading of the Ode, For the Fallen by R. L. Binyon, published in September 1914, read first in Te Reo Māori by Deputy Head Prefect Matayas Thomson, then in English by Senior Prefect Xavier Clark. The Last Post and Rouse performed on the trumpet by senior student Jonathan Chang from the C Floor was, as always, an emotional end to the service.

In his address, Dr Etty - who holds a PhD in History - discussed the enduring relationship between education, service, and sacrifice, using the history of Auckland Grammar School as its central lens.

Beginning with the reforms of educationalist George Hogben, Dr Etty emphasises the longstanding belief that "schools had a moral responsibility; a responsibility to develop in young people the habits and character that would shape their lives, but also the future of society." For George Hogben and his contemporaries, this meant instilling values such as discipline, patriotism, and service, qualities that would later define a generation called to war. "This patriotic urge to enlist motivated volunteers all over the world. The excitement for war seemed odd in the years afterwards."

Dr Etty's address focused particularly on the experiences of Grammar masters and Old Boys during both World Wars. Teachers such as James Dinneen and Frederick Airey are remembered not only for their service in the classroom, but also for their leadership and sacrifice on the battlefield. Dr Etty noted that Dinneen left his teaching role to serve as a captain and died following the Battle of the Somme in 1916. "In the report on his death published in the School Chronicle, a private who served with James Dinneen described him as 'strict and conscientious', a 'gallant officer' and someone who did more for his men than any other officer. His service to his country was matched by his service to his comrades."

Drawing on examples from ancient societies, Dr Etty highlighted that education has historically combined intellectual and physical development to prepare individuals for leadership in all aspects of life. He referred to education theorist Gert Biesta: "Education has three purposes: the transmission of knowledge and skills, induction into existing cultural traditions, and the development of individuals capable of independent thought and responsible action."

In a contemporary context, Dr Etty acknowledges that today's students face a complex and uncertain world shaped by global challenges. As such, the role of education in fostering sound judgement, resilience, and a commitment to democratic values is more important than ever. He called upon the School community to remember those who served.

"Teaching, like fighting, is an act of faith. Faith that the future remains open. Faith that it matters enough to commit a lifetime to. Faith that the formation of young people represents society's best hope. That is how this school remembers these men - not only as soldiers, but also as teachers. We remember what they understood: that forming the next generation, and fighting for the next generation, are among the most serious things a person can do. That it was worth their lives' work to do it well. And that many of them gave their lives in the process."

Studying A Level History, Senior Prefect Rupert Glaetzer's Anzac address focused on Grammar's impact on the war and the valiant and selfless sacrifice of those Grammar boys who fought in the World Wars. He spoke about the harrowing cost of World War I. "New Zealand sent more men to fight per head of population than any other nation, yet 18,166 souls never returned home, more than 500 of them dying from disease in training camps, before they even saw the frontline of battle."

He told those gathered about Sapper Richard Hobbs Beamish, a bright student who started at Grammar in 1908 in 3A. Richard was a sharp target shooter and after moving to England in 1912, he enlisted with the New Zealand Engineers, beginning basic training in Egypt. Sadly, he was sent home ill to New Zealand in 1916 and died a year later from tuberculosis in 1917, aged just 23 years old.

The names on the plaques surrounding the War Memorial of Old Boys who perished in World War II were added in 1953, honouring 400 Grammar men who gave their lives for their country. Among them was Sergeant Lauritz Andrew Woodney (Laurie) Rasmussen.

Rupert told of his schooling life before being called to serve. "Entering Auckland Grammar School in 1936, Laurie was just like us, a student with a fine academic record who spent his weekends playing club Cricket for Grafton and Rugby for the Grammar Old Boys. But when the world went to war, he traded the classroom for the cockpit. He became the youngest New Zealander to serve in the Battle of Britain, tasked with protecting the skies over England."

Sharing of Laurie's "hauntingly brief" time as a soldier on the frontline was confronting. "Just 19 hours after being appointed an air gunner, Laurie climbed into his Defiant aircraft for his first mission, an interception patrol. Tragically, the plane never gained altitude, striking the ground shortly after takeoff. Laurie never got to finish that mission. He was a boy who left our classrooms to defend our freedom. He was just 18 years old."

Rupert also spoke of his personal connection to Anzac Day. "My great-grandfather fought in World War II with the Royal Australian Air Force. So standing here today, I am reminded that Anzac Day has never belonged to one nation alone. The bond between Australia and New Zealand was forged from the trenches at Gallipoli to the skies in Britain. This was sustained through common sacrifice and generations of service."

Rupert then drew attention to those Old Boys of Auckland Grammar School who never returned from war. "The men we remember today were students, teachers, sons, brothers, and friends. They all had futures that were full of possibility and dreams. Futures they willingly set aside for something greater than themselves. As students, we inherit not only a fine education, but a responsibility: to remember, to respect, and to uphold the values they stood for."

Rupert concluded his address by emphasising the importance of remembering those who have gone before us: "Today, as we stand in silence, may we remember that the freedoms we enjoy were not free. They were hard earned, by ordinary people, people like us, people who showed extraordinary courage when the world and New Zealand needed it. Lest we forget."

Old Boys' Association

Augusta and Lion Award Nominations

We are now accepting nominations for the annual Auckland Grammar School Old Boys' Association Augusta Awards for 2026. Candidates will be considered for two types of awards - the Augusta Award and the Lion Award, introduced in 2018. These will be presented at the annual Augusta Awards Dinner on Thursday 5 November 2026.

Augusta Awards. The annual Augusta Awards evolved from the Association's Old Boy of the Year Award. They are awarded to Old Boys who are at the top of their respective industries or specialties, or who have demonstrated a remarkable contribution to the Arts, Business, Sport and/or Public Service.

More specifically, they are intended to acknowledge Old Boys who have made a significant contribution in their chosen field, who have made a positive impact in their field throughout their careers and who have used their talents to encourage and inspire others to follow their lead. The 2025 Augusta Awards were presented by former Headmaster John Morris, ONZM to Dr Murray MacCormick, CNZM '59, David Morgan, ONZM '73 and Richard Chambers '85.

History — Serving in the 1940s

The Remarkable Survival of Tim Hill '34

David Hill reflects on the tenacious character of his Godfather, Trevor Neville 'Tim' Hill '34 (no relation). It was a trait that helped him survive WW II. Tim Hill '34 attended Auckland Grammar School, as did his brothers Graham '33, Ross '36 and Bruce '46. He held a distinguished sporting record, winning the School steeplechase, the half mile, and three weights in the school boxing championships, and playing 1st XV Rugby in 1938. Athleticism was prized in the family, with his father George Hill '03 competing in Athletics at the Stockholm Olympics in 1912.

With the outbreak of war, Tim enlisted in the Royal Navy Volunteer Reserve (A1887) in March 1940. After his initial training at the New Zealand naval base at Devonport, Tim was sent to the UK for training, then to the 'Alynbank' - a merchant ship converted into a floating anti-aircraft defence vessel. After eight months he was sent to the Coastal Defence at Fort William in Scotland for training in small boat handling and gunnery, then was drafted to join a Flotilla being assembled in defence of Singapore. Tim's convoy left Britain on 12 November 1941, and by the time it reached Singapore on 14 January 1942, the Malayan Campaign was virtually lost and the enemy was within artillery range of the city.

Tim served on ML311, a Fairmile patrol boat delivering commandos and rescuing retreating soldiers. When the moment came to evacuate the beleaguered city, ML311 and another Fairmile, ML310 - commanded by fellow Old Boy Sub-Lt Johnny Bull '28 - were tasked with evacuating members of the General Staff. None of the 60-plus vessels that attempted to escape Singapore on Friday 13 February 1942 survived. ML311 was intercepted and blown out of the water; of over 50 men on board, barely 14 escaped with their lives. Tim Hill was among them.

After managing to get ashore, he spent five days hiding in mangroves. Supported by villagers, a group attempted to escape in a lifeboat that had been washed ashore, making their way cautiously along the Sumatran coast until they were again attacked by the enemy. The survivors, including Tim, rowed on, evading the enemy, crossing the Sunda Strait to Java, where he was finally captured and became a prisoner of war. This story is told by one of the men involved, Australian Rohan Rivett, in his book 'Behind Bamboo.' As a POW, Tim survived the notorious Changi Prison camp and later the Burma-Thailand 'Death Railway', in the camp with the highest death rates due to disease, malnutrition and brutal treatment. His survival was likely due to his competitive nature and physical resilience.

When Tim was repatriated in 1946, his sweetheart was standing on the Wellington wharf with a wedding dress over her arm. They were married forthwith. Peter Stanes '58, the School's Archivist/Historian, has compiled a list of the Old Boys who served with the Navy and Merchant Navy in WW II who were not so fortunate. 42 Old Boys are sadly on that list. Lest we forget.

Grammar and "The Ashes"

The connection between the School and the famous cricket urn.

How, you might well think, does the School have anything to do with that symbol of Australian/English cricket rivalry, "The Ashes". The link comes through Samuel Percy Jones, who was born in Sydney, Australia, on 1 August 1861. From his early days at Sydney Grammar School, Jones showed considerable cricket ability as both a batsman and a bowler.

He made his test debut in 1881 against England in Australia and, not quite 21 years old, he toured England in the Australian team in 1882, when they defeated England for the first time on English soil at the Kennington Oval. Australia was bowled out for only 63 runs in the first innings, Jones scoring a duck. On the second day, Jones was run out for 6 - and, assuming the ball was dead, left his crease to pat down the pitch and was promptly run out by the famous W. G. Grace. The Australians were incensed at the unfairness of it.

After the innings, the Australian fast bowler Fred Spofforth, the 'Demon Bowler', informed Grace that his gesture would "cost you the match". Sure enough, England were all out for 77, 8 runs short of a win. The next day, journalist Reginald Shirley Brooks published a mock 'obituary': "In Affectionate Remembrance of English Cricket, which died at the Oval on 29th August, 1882... N.B. - The body will be cremated and the ashes taken to Australia."

An English team, captained by Ivo Bligh, toured Australia in late 1882, vowing to return the mythical "Ashes" to England. Having won the series, he was presented with a small urn, containing ashes which may have been of a bail, a ball or even a lady's shawl. On his death, the urn was presented to the Marylebone Cricket Club (MCC) at Lord's, where it resides in the museum today.

Jones went on to tour England for Australia in 1886, 1888 and 1890, twice hospitalised - once with smallpox, so seriously that an ambiguous message back to Australia resulted in his death being reported.

So, how does this story relate to Auckland Grammar School? Jones moved to New Zealand, arriving in Auckland on 7 November 1904, employed as cricket coach at the School. In addition to coaching, Jones was appointed Clerk of the School in 1916, retaining the position until his retirement in 1935. Sammy Jones died in Auckland on 14 July 1951, aged 89, leaving his Kiwi wife and one son.
